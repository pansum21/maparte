The Where We Met Map: A Complete Guide to a Place Worth Framing

How to turn the exact spot where two people met into a map poster: finding the place, framing it, choosing a theme and writing the words.

Most couples can tell you exactly where they met, and most can tell you in so much detail that you feel you were there. The bench by the river. The corner of the third floor corridor. The queue for the last train. The gate at an airport in a city neither of them lived in. The place is rarely grand. It is the setting of a small moment that turned out to matter, and that is exactly why it makes such a good poster.

A "where we met" map takes that place and turns it into a piece of art: a map of the neighbourhood or the city, framed around the exact spot, with the names or the date underneath. It is one of the most popular personal map gifts, for anniversaries, weddings, engagements, birthdays and for no reason at all. This guide is a full, practical walk through making one well: choosing the place, framing it, picking colours and a shape, writing the words, and giving it in a way that lands.

We make Maparte, a browser tool for map posters and wallpapers, and the steps below use its real options. Maparte is digital only. You design in the browser, look at a 4K preview and download a file. We do not print or ship anything, so we will also be clear about how to turn the file into a printed gift when you want one. Wherever we give example captions, names or dates, they are illustrations made up to show an idea. They are not real customers. Please replace them with your own details.

The short version. Find the exact place. Frame it so the story is readable: a close view for a street corner, a wide view for a whole city. Choose a theme that matches the mood. Keep the words short and check every date. Preview at full size. Then decide how to give it: a screen, a printed poster, or a wallpaper that arrives today.

Why this gift works

A map has a quality that few other gifts share: it is both abstract and specific. A pattern of lines looks beautiful from across the room, and yet every line is a real street. When the person you give it to looks closely, they find the building, the junction, the bridge. The gift rewards attention, and attention is what the gift is about.

There are three reasons it works so well for couples.

It records a fact only you two know. A bought gift can be given to anyone. A map of one specific corner can only be given to one person. Even if a friend sees it on the wall, they will not recognise the spot, and that private meaning is part of its charm.

It ages well. A photo can feel dated. A map of the place where you met stays accurate as a memory, even if the shops on that street change. Over time the poster becomes a record of a version of the place that no longer exists, which makes it more precious, not less.

It can be quiet or bold. A pale, muted theme with thin lines fits a calm bedroom. A deep navy with gold lines makes a statement above a sofa. The same place can carry either mood, so the gift can be matched to the person's taste.

What counts as "where we met"

You do not need a romantic film setting. In fact, the less glamorous the place, the more personal the result. Think about the following kinds of places, and notice which one is yours.

  • A school, college or university campus. Maps of campuses have a distinctive look, with winding paths and large green areas.
  • A workplace or a street near it. The cafe where everyone had lunch, the bus stop outside the office.
  • A neighbourhood or a building. The flat share, the shared stairwell, the street where you were neighbours.
  • A city you were both visiting. A hostel, a train station, a festival, a tour group.
  • A wedding or a party. The venue where a mutual friend introduced you.
  • A place on a trip. A mountain trail, a beach, an island, a market.
  • A place that no longer exists. A building that was knocked down, a cafe that closed. The map still shows the street, and the gap is part of the meaning.
  • A place you only met online, but then finally visited. The first address you met at in person. In a long-distance story, this is often the true "where we met".

If you were both in several of these, choose the one that tells the story best, or make a set. We come back to sets near the end.

One place, or two?

Many couples have two places: where they first met and where they first knew they were a couple. These are often different. One poster can show only one. If you want to mark both, there are two options. You can make two matching posters, one for each place. Or you can make a single wider view that includes both places, with a marker at each, and a short line in the caption explaining which is which.

Finding the exact spot

You might think you know where you met, and then discover you are not sure of the street. Before opening any tool, spend ten minutes collecting evidence. This is part of the fun, and it often turns up stories you had forgotten.

  • Look at old photos. Photos taken on a phone often carry location information. Open the photo's details and see whether a place is recorded.
  • Read old messages. The first conversation may mention a street, a bar or a station.
  • Check the calendar or tickets. A booking confirmation, an event ticket or a train ticket gives you an address.
  • Ask the other person, or a friend who was there. Be careful if it is a surprise, and ask in a way that does not give the game away.
  • Look at a map. Scroll around until the shapes remind you. Often you recognise a junction before you remember its name.

Write down the place as an address or as a pair of coordinates. Coordinates are two numbers, latitude and longitude, that identify a point on the Earth. Most map tools show them when you tap and hold on a spot. They are worth writing down, because you can type them straight into the Maparte editor, and they also make a lovely detail on the finished poster.

Framing: how close, how far?

Framing decides what the poster says. A map zoomed in on a single junction says "this exact spot". A map zoomed out to show a whole city says "this city, and the part of it that was ours". Both are good, and they feel quite different.

The close view

A close view shows streets, building blocks and small details. It is ideal when the place itself is small and precise: a corner, a campus, a bridge. Put the exact spot in the middle, and keep enough surrounding streets that the map still looks like a map and not a close-up of a few lines. A marker, such as a pin or a heart, on the spot makes the meaning clear.

The wide view

A wide view shows the whole city or town, and it is the best choice when the story is about the place as a whole, such as the city you both moved to, or the city where you met on a trip. The streets become a texture, and the shapes of rivers, coastlines and parks stand out. At this scale you will want to keep the title large and the text simple, because the map is doing most of the work. A small marker on the exact spot still works, and it gives the viewer a pleasant moment of discovery when they find it.

If you are unsure, make both. The editor keeps your settings, and changing the view takes a minute. Put the two previews side by side and the right one usually announces itself.

Framing the view well

Whichever scale you choose, a few habits improve the result.

  • Put the key spot a little above the centre. The eye tends to rest slightly above the middle, and the bottom of the poster often has a caption.
  • Include one big shape. A river, a coastline, a park or a hill gives the map a structure. If the exact spot is near water, make sure the water is in the frame.
  • Avoid awkward cuts. If a road or a river is cut off by the edge at a strange angle, nudge the view a little.
  • Look at it small. Zoom out and squint. If the shape still looks good as a small thumbnail, it will look good on the wall.

Choosing a theme that matches the story

The theme is the palette and the line style of the map. It sets the mood before anyone reads a word. Maparte has 22 themes, and for a personal poster it helps to think in moods rather than names. Here are the moods that fit a "where we met" story, with the themes that deliver them. You can see every theme on a real city in our themes guide, linked at the end.

Warm and romantic. Blush, Sunset, Ruby and Terracotta lean towards warm tones. Blush is soft and gentle. Sunset glows. Ruby is dramatic and deep, with rich reds over a dark ground. Terracotta is earthy and welcoming. These suit anniversaries, engagements and weddings.

Quiet and elegant. Carrara, Pencil Sketch and Sandstone are calm, with off-white backgrounds and restrained lines. They look good in a bedroom or a hallway and they sit comfortably with wood and linen. Choose these if the person likes understated things.

Deep and dramatic. Midnight Blue, Noir and Blueprint are dark and bold. Midnight Blue pairs navy with gold and feels like an evening. Noir is graphic and strong. Blueprint suggests an architect's drawing and works well for a city with a strong grid. They are good for statement walls, and they look excellent on screens.

Natural and calm. Sage, Ocean and Blue Water bring greens and blues. Sage is a muted green that is easy to live with. Ocean and Blue Water make water the hero, which is perfect if you met by a river, a harbour or a coast.

Playful. Crayon Art, Pop Art and Chalkboard Cafe are lively. They suit a story that began with laughter, a party or a student flat share.

Artistic. Watercolor Wash, Japanese Ink, Retro Vintage, Neon and Aurora each have a strong personality. Japanese Ink is clean and airy with a single accent. Retro Vintage feels like an old atlas. Neon and Aurora glow on a dark background.

A simple way to choose

Do not choose by name. Choose by comparing. Set up the view of your place, then click through five or six themes without changing anything else. Note which one makes you say "that's it". Then check with these questions.

  1. Does it suit the room? Look at the colours of the wall where the poster will hang, the sofa, the cushions. A theme that echoes one of them fits comfortably.
  2. Does it suit the person? The best gifts reflect the receiver's taste, not the giver's. If you are not sure, look at what they already wear and own.
  3. Does it read from a distance? Step back from your screen. Do the main roads still show? Can you see the water?
  4. Will you still like it in a year? The loudest options are exciting on day one. Calmer ones tend to grow on you.

If two themes tie, pick the calmer one. A poster is a long-term resident.

Choosing a layout

The layout is the shape and size of the finished image. Maparte has 79 layouts in six groups: 52 print formats, 3 double-frame compositions, 10 social media shapes, 7 wallpaper layouts, 4 iPhone layouts and 3 web layouts. For a "where we met" gift, a handful are most useful.

  • A4 Portrait (21 x 29.7 cm). The default, and a good all-round choice. It fits a standard frame, prints on a home printer and works on a screen.
  • Square Caption A4. A layout with a square map and a caption area below. It is designed for exactly this kind of poster, where the map is the picture and a few lines of text tell the story.
  • A3 Portrait or 12 x 18 inches. A larger wall size, good for a living room.
  • Poster 50 x 70 cm (B2) or 24 x 36 inches. A big statement piece.
  • Instagram Square (1080 x 1080 px). A square image for sharing the story with friends, or for a message.
  • Story (9:16, 1080 x 1920 px). A tall image for stories on social platforms.
  • iPhone and Galaxy wallpaper layouts. A map of the place as a lock screen. For recent iPhones, the 16/17 Pro and iPhone 17 layout is 1206 x 2622 pixels.

If you plan to frame it, measure the frame first and choose the layout that matches. If you plan to give it only as a file, choose the square or the phone layout, and consider making both. A person who receives a wallpaper of the place where you met, and then later a printed poster of the same place, gets the same gift twice, in two forms.

Borders, text and the details that make it look designed

Once the place, theme and layout are right, a few details turn a nice map into a designed poster.

Border. Maparte offers 13 border styles, and you can adjust line thickness and spacing. A thin single line or a double line gives a classic, framed look. No border is cleaner for a phone wallpaper. If you plan to put the poster in a frame with a mat, a thin border is plenty, because the mat is already a border.

Title. The place name is the classic title. For a personal poster, you can use a different title, such as the names of the two people, a short phrase or a single word. If the place name is long or unusual, let it stand alone, and put the names in the caption.

Subtitle. Use it for the country, region or neighbourhood, or for a short line.

Details. The editor lets you add lines of detail. Coordinates are a favourite: they look good, they are exact, and they are a quiet clue that the place is special. The date is the other favourite.

Typeface. Maparte offers a list of typefaces, grouped as serif, sans serif, display and monospace. A serif face suits a classic, formal poster. A clean sans face suits a modern one. Whichever you choose, use one typeface throughout.

Markers. The Markers panel lets you add a pin, an icon or an emoji, and supports custom icons. A single small marker on the exact spot is usually the right amount. Several markers can tell a route through the story: where you met, where you had your first coffee, where you first said "I love you".

Writing the words

Plain captions are often the strongest. A date, two names and nothing else. Here are some ideas, grouped by tone. They are examples to adapt, not text to copy.

Plain. "Anna and Rahul", "14 February 2020", "Where it started".

Warm. "The corner where our story began", "Right here, by chance", "Exactly here".

Playful. "You spilled coffee on my map", "We both pretended not to notice", "The bus was late. Thank goodness."

Formal. "Latitude and longitude, then the date". A set of coordinates followed by a month and a year looks like a label from a museum.

Short and silent. Nothing but the map and a tiny date. If the place and the person are enough, the quietest poster may be the best.

Avoid anything you have to explain. If the recipient needs to ask what a line means, it is too clever. Keep dates in the format you use at home, and check each digit. We cover proofreading in the checklist below.

Step by step: making it in the Maparte editor

Here is the whole process, in the order that works best. It takes about fifteen to thirty minutes the first time.

  1. Open the editor at maparte.com. Type the place name in the location field, or enter the coordinates you collected. The map moves to that spot.
  2. Frame the view. Pan and zoom until the exact spot sits a little above the centre, and until a big shape such as a river or park is in the frame.
  3. Choose the layout. If you have a frame, choose its size. If you do not, start with A4 Portrait or Square Caption A4.
  4. Choose a theme. Click through five or six and compare on the same view. Pick the one that matches the mood and the room.
  5. Add a border. Try a thin line or a double line, or leave it off.
  6. Edit the text. Write the title, the subtitle and one or two detail lines. Read each aloud.
  7. Add a marker. Place a small pin or icon on the exact spot. Keep it small.
  8. Check the 4K preview. Zoom in on the corners, the thin lines and the text. Look at the data credit, which should be present and small.
  9. Download. Open the download panel and choose a format. PNG is a safe general choice. Check the pixel size stated for your layout. Save the file in two places.

If the design is a surprise, do this on your own device, and hide the file in a folder with a plain name.

Reading the map you are about to give

A map poster looks simple, but a little knowledge of how it is drawn helps you choose a view that shows your place at its best.

Roads have a hierarchy. On a well-drawn map, main roads are thicker and darker, secondary roads are thinner, and small streets are the finest lines. When you zoom out, the finest lines blend into a texture, and the main roads form a skeleton. When you zoom in, the small streets become clear individual lines. For a "where we met" poster, this means a close view shows the corner and its neighbouring streets in detail, while a wide view shows the skeleton of the city.

Water and green space give a place its character. Rivers, lakes, coasts and parks are drawn as areas of colour. They are often the first thing the eye notices. If your spot is near water or a park, put it in the frame, and choose a theme in which it reads clearly.

Not every place is mapped to the same level of detail. The map data comes from OpenStreetMap contributors. It is rich in most towns and cities, and sparser in some rural areas. A small village with few mapped roads can still make a lovely poster, because each line has room to breathe. If the place you want has little detail, zoom out a little to include a river, a coast or a larger town for context.

The map shows the place as it is now. If a building was knocked down or a street renamed, the poster shows the current state. If that is part of your story, you can use the caption to say what stood there before.

A tip for common place names

Many towns share a name. Before you decide, check the country or region next to the place name in the search results, and look at the map to confirm that it is the one you meant. A poster of the wrong place with the right name is a poor surprise.

A tip for the exact corner

If the exact corner is not a named place, use coordinates. Open a map app, press and hold on the spot, and copy the two numbers shown. Type them into the editor's search, and the view jumps to the spot. Then add a small marker on it.

Making it for a long-distance couple

A long-distance story has its own version of "where we met". It might be the airport arrivals hall where you first stood in the same place, or a cafe in a city neither of you lived in. Here are some ways to make that poster feel right.

  • Use a wide view that includes both home cities. If they are close enough, a single map can show both, with a marker at each and a third at the meeting place. If they are too far apart, make two posters, one for each place, and give one to each person.
  • Make matching copies. Because the poster is a file, each of you can print a copy in your own country, and hang them on the same day.
  • Choose a calm theme. A long-distance poster is often a quiet, longing sort of thing. A soft or cool theme suits it.
  • Use the date of the first meeting. Not the date you met online, but the day you were in the same room.
  • Give it as a wallpaper first. A phone wallpaper arrives instantly, and it is a small daily reminder of a place you both know.

Where to hang it

The best place for a "where we met" poster is somewhere you pass often and pause. Here are some ideas.

  • Above a bed. A larger poster, in a calm theme, looks good in a bedroom.
  • In a hallway. A place you see at the start and end of every day.
  • Beside a desk. A smaller poster, close enough to read the caption.
  • On a gallery wall. Combined with photos and prints of different sizes. Use a simple frame and a mat to bring the pieces together.
  • On a shelf. Lean a frame against a wall, with a few books. It is easy to change and needs no nails.

Consider the light. A poster in strong direct sun fades over time, and a glass frame in a bright window reflects. A spot with soft, even light shows the lines best.

Ten places, ten ways to frame them

Abstract advice is easier to use with examples. The scenarios below are illustrations. The people and places are made up to show how the choices work, and they are not real customers. Use them as a pattern, then swap in your own story.

The university corner

Two students met in a queue outside a lecture hall. The campus is small, so a close view works: the buildings are blocks, the paths wind between lawns, and the exact door is visible. A pale theme with thin dark lines keeps the campus airy. A single heart marker on the door. The caption has the first term and the year. Because campus maps have a lot of green, a green or sage theme makes the lawns the hero.

The late-night station

A couple met on a delayed train, on a platform neither of them was supposed to be on. A station is a node where many lines meet, so a medium view that shows the rails fanning out feels right. A dark theme gives the map the feeling of a night journey, and a thin gold line on navy looks like an evening departure board. The caption can be the time of the delay, as a joke only they understand.

The whole city, from far away

Two people moved to the same large city in the same month and met at a friend's flat. Their story is about the city, not a single corner. A wide view that shows the whole urban shape, the river that cuts through it and the green belt around it, makes a strong poster. A small marker shows the district where they met. Keep the text minimal: the city name, and a date. This is also the view that looks best from across a room, because the pattern of the city does the work.

The island holiday

A pair met on a ferry. The map is mostly water, and that is its beauty: a small island, a pale sea and a few thin roads. A water-forward theme such as Ocean or Blue Water makes the sea the star. A wide view with generous margins feels calm. The caption can be a pair of coordinates and a month.

The old neighbourhood

A couple who grew up two streets apart met properly only as adults. A map that includes both homes tells a double story. Put a marker on each, and a small line in the caption. A warm theme such as Terracotta or Sandstone suits the idea of home.

The market and the street food stall

A long queue and one stall. Neighbourhood markets have dense street grids, and a close view captures them. A playful theme gives it the right spirit, and a caption such as "Table for two, no table" can make a smile.

The mountain trail

Two hikers met at a trailhead. A map with contour feeling, a strong river and a sense of altitude works well with an earthy or forest-green theme. If you have the trail as a track file, you can show it as a line. We explain this in our guide to route posters. A marker on the trailhead and a second on the summit tell the story of the day.

The wedding venue

Where they met may be where a friend got married. The map of the venue and its surroundings can be kept formal: thin border, a serif title and a date. A classic, neutral theme looks right on a hallway wall.

The long-distance first meeting

Many couples first met online, and the first time they stood in the same place was at an airport arrivals hall or a cafe in a third city. That place is their real "where we met". A wide map with a marker on that spot, plus a faint second marker for each home city if the view allows, tells a long-distance story with great economy.

A place that has changed

The cafe closed years ago, or the building has been replaced. A map shows the street as it is now, and the gap is part of the meaning. In the caption, you can write the old name of the place. The poster becomes a record of something that no longer exists.

Ways to give it

How you give a map poster matters almost as much as the poster. Here are options that match different situations.

On a screen, today. Because a digital file exists the moment you download it, you can send it as a message on the day. This is ideal when you are far apart, when you forgot a date, or when you want to give a preview and then follow up with a printed version.

As a wallpaper. Choose the phone layout and set it on their lock screen when they are not looking, or send it with a note. It becomes part of their day, every time they pick up the phone.

Printed and framed. Take the file to a print shop of your choice, or use a home printer for a small size. Ask for a paper with a matte finish for a calm look. We do not print or ship posters, so this step is yours. Our guide to choosing a map poster maker has a list of questions to ask a print shop, and the printing section below has more.

Folded in a card. For a small A5 or A4 print, a map folded into a card with a handwritten line inside is intimate and simple.

As a set. Two posters, one of each place, give you a diptych. Keep the theme, layout and typeface the same, and change only the place and the words.

With a story. However you give it, tell the story. Hand it over and say: "Do you know where this is?" The moment of recognition is the gift.

Printing it yourself, briefly

If you want paper, you choose the print route. For small prints, a home printer on good paper is fine. For larger sizes, a local print shop will have a better machine and a wider choice of paper. A few practical points.

  • Send the file at its original size. Do not resize or crop it.
  • Ask for a small test print first, because screens and paper show colour differently.
  • Choose a matte paper if you want a quiet look, and a satin or semi-gloss paper if you want the colours to feel deeper.
  • Check that the frame matches the proportions of the layout you chose.

A proofreading checklist

A map poster is a small piece of text on a large piece of image, and a mistake on it is hard to forgive. Go through this list before you download, and again on the downloaded file.

  1. Spell every word aloud. Do it slowly, letter by letter, for names.
  2. Check every digit. Dates, coordinates, years. Read each number twice.
  3. Check the date format. Day first or month first? Use the one your recipient uses.
  4. Look at the exact spot. Zoom into the preview and confirm the marker sits on the right corner.
  5. Look at the edges. Make sure no text is cut off and no road ends awkwardly at the border.
  6. Look at the credit line. The data credit should be there and legible, and it should not overlap other text.
  7. Ask someone else to read it. Fresh eyes find what yours skip, as long as the surprise survives.
  8. Download and open the file. Open it from the folder, not the browser, and zoom to full size.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing the wrong scale. A whole-city view for a story that belongs to a single corner, or the reverse.
  • Too many markers. Three or four are plenty. Ten becomes a cluttered chart.
  • Text that explains too much. Let the map speak.
  • Mixing typefaces. One typeface per poster is a good rule.
  • A theme that clashes with the room. Check the wall colour.
  • Leaving it to the last day. Print shops get busy before holidays. Allow time for a test print.
  • Forgetting the layout. A poster made for A4 will not fill a different frame without cropping.
  • Skipping the full-size check. The 4K preview exists for a reason. Use it.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use the exact coordinates?

Yes. You can type coordinates into the location field, and you can also place the coordinates as a line of text on the poster.

Can I make a poster for a place that does not exist any more?

You can make a map of where it used to be. The map shows the current streets. The caption can tell the rest.

Which theme is best for a wedding or an engagement?

There is no single best choice. Soft warm themes and deep dramatic ones are both popular. Compare three on your own place and think about the room.

How big should I print it?

Choose by the wall. A4 suits a desk, a shelf or a small frame. A3 suits a hallway or a bedroom. 50 x 70 cm suits a living room. Check the pixel size stated for the layout before you decide.

Can I make two posters that match?

Yes. Keep the theme, layout, typeface and text position the same, and change the place and the words. Write the settings down so you can repeat them.

Does Maparte print or ship posters?

No. Maparte provides digital files only. You download a file and use it on a screen, share it or take it to a printer of your choice.

Which formats can I download?

The live download panel lists PNG, JPG, TIFF, a PNG and JPG pack, PDF, SVG and an all-in-one master bundle. Raster files go up to 300 pixels per inch. Always check the live panel for the current options and terms.

Can I add a photo of us?

The editor's wall preview lets you see the poster in a room scene. If you want a photo as well, you can combine the poster file with a photo in a frame or a collage by other means. The poster itself is a map.

Do I need an account?

You can try the editor in the browser and see the preview. Check the live site for the current download steps.

What if I do not know the exact place?

Collect evidence first: old photos, messages and tickets often hold the answer. A whole-city view is a safe choice when the exact spot is uncertain.

Is the map accurate?

The map data comes from OpenStreetMap contributors, and is detailed in most towns and cities. It shows the place as it is now. The poster is a work of art and is not meant for navigation.

Final thoughts

A "where we met" map works because it is small and exact. It takes one moment in one place and gives it a shape you can hang on a wall or carry in your pocket. Find the spot, frame it so the story is readable, choose a theme that matches the mood, write only words you have checked, and look at the preview at full size.

When you are ready, open the Maparte editor at maparte.com, type in the place, and try three themes. If you want ideas for the colours, read our guide to the themes. If your story involves a route, such as a first walk or a long run, read our guide to route posters. And if you are making a gift for a first anniversary, our guide to the first-anniversary map gift is just as practical.

The best version of this poster is not the fanciest. It is the one that makes the person you give it to stop, look closer and smile.
