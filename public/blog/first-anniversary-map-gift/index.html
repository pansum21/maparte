First Anniversary Map Gift: Ideas, Steps and Words

A first anniversary is a strange milestone. It is the first time you look back over a whole year together and notice how much has happened: the first holiday, the first argument about the dishwasher, the first time you knew which mug was theirs. A good gift for that day does not need to be expensive or grand. It needs to say, quietly, "I remember".

A map is one of the best ways to say that. It can show the place where you met, the street of your first flat, the city where you got married, the route of a trip, or the town where one of you grew up and the other visited for the first time. It looks like art and it reads like a story. And because the traditional gift for a first anniversary is paper, a poster is a rare gift that is both modern and traditional at once.

This guide is a full, practical walk through making a first-anniversary map gift: choosing which place to show, how to frame it, which theme and layout to choose, what to write, how to give it, and how to plan the timing so that it is ready on the day. We make Maparte, a browser tool for map posters and wallpapers, and the steps use its real options. Maparte is digital only. You design in the browser, check a 4K preview and download a file. We do not print or ship anything, so we are also clear about what to do when you want the poster on paper. Example captions, names and dates in this guide are illustrations made up to show an idea. They are not real customers, so please replace them with your own details.

The short version. Pick one place that tells your first year. Frame it so the story is readable. Choose a theme to match the mood and the room. Write only words you have checked, such as two names and a date. Preview at full size. Then plan how to give it: a file on the day, a printed poster, or both. Start a week or two before, so that you have time for a test print if you want paper.

Why a map suits the first year

Gift lists for a first anniversary tend to repeat the same ideas: flowers, a dinner, a photo book, a piece of jewellery. All of these are lovely. A map has three qualities that set it apart.

It is personal without being awkward. A photo of the two of you says "us". A map of a place says "us, here". That is a quieter and more grown-up way to say the same thing, and it works for people who dislike seeing their own face on the wall.

It gives the year a shape. A year is hard to hold in the mind. A map gives it a location. When you look at it, you can point to a spot and say "that is where it began".

It works for any couple. There is no required level of romance. A poster with a plain title and a date can be as warm as one with a poem, and it can be matched to a minimalist flat or a cluttered bookshelf.

The paper tradition, lightly

In many cultures, anniversaries are linked to materials, and the first year is traditionally linked to paper. Paper is thin and delicate, and it suggests a relationship at the beginning of a long book. It is a pleasant idea to build a gift around, and a printed poster fits it exactly. You do not need to take the tradition seriously for the idea to be useful. A line on the card, such as "Year one, on paper", is a small, charming link.

If you give only the digital file, the paper idea still works: you can promise a printed copy, and print it later. Many people give the file on the day and a framed print a few weeks later, and enjoy the gift twice.

Choose the story first

Before you open any tool, decide what story the map tells. Here are the most common choices for a first anniversary, and the situations they suit.

  • Where we met. The classic. A close view of the spot, or a wide view of the city. We have a separate guide on this idea, with a full method.
  • Where we got engaged or married. A venue, a park, a beach, a city hall. A formal theme and a date make this one feel like a certificate.
  • Where we live now. The flat or the street where your life together began. This is the most intimate version: it shows your own front door.
  • Where we went on our first trip. A city, an island, a mountain. A wide view with a marker for the place you stayed.
  • Where we spent the day. A route through a city: the place you met for coffee, the park you walked through, the place you had dinner. A few markers tell a day.
  • Two homes. A map that shows where each of you grew up, with a marker on each. Good for long-distance relationships and for couples from different countries.
  • A route we ran, walked or cycled. If you did something together, such as a half marathon or a long walk, the route makes a striking poster. We have a guide for route posters.

If you cannot choose, ask which one you would talk about for ten minutes if a stranger asked. That is the story.

One year, many places

Many couples find that the year has too many places for one poster. A first anniversary map can be a set of three small posters that hang together, one for each of three milestones, or a single wider map with markers at each. A set looks very good on a wall and gives you a reason to come back the next year with a fourth.

How to find the details

A first-anniversary gift is a good moment to collect the details of the year. Use these sources, and enjoy the walk down memory lane.

  • Photos. Phone photos often have a location in their details. Scroll back through the year and find the first photo at each place.
  • Messages. Search your messages for a place name, a street or a station.
  • Calendar. Bookings, tickets and reminders hold dates and addresses.
  • Receipts and confirmations. A hotel or restaurant booking gives an exact address.
  • The other person's friends. Be careful with a surprise, but a trusted friend can often fill in a detail.

Write the place as an address or as coordinates, which are two numbers that identify a point on the Earth. Most map apps show them when you tap and hold on a spot. Maparte lets you type them in, and they also make a lovely detail on the poster.

Framing: close or far

Framing decides what the poster says. A close view shows streets and buildings and says "this exact spot". A wide view shows a whole city or a coastline and says "this place, and our part of it".

Close view. Best for a corner, a building, a park or a campus. Put the spot a little above the centre of the frame and keep enough surrounding streets that the map still looks like a map. A small marker, such as a pin or a heart, makes the meaning clear.

Wide view. Best when the story is about a city, an island or a region. The streets become a texture, and the shapes of rivers, coastlines and parks stand out. Keep the title large and the text simple. A tiny marker on the exact spot gives the viewer a pleasant moment of discovery.

Some habits help in either case. Include one big shape such as a river or a coast, because it gives the map a structure. Avoid awkward cuts where a road is sliced at a strange angle by the edge. And look at the view small: if it still looks good as a thumbnail, it will look good on a wall.

If you are not sure, make both views. The editor keeps your settings, and changing the view takes a minute. Compare them side by side.

Choosing the theme

The theme is the palette and line style of the map. Maparte has 21 themes, and for an anniversary it helps to think in moods.

  • Warm and romantic. Blush, Sunset, Ruby and Terracotta lean warm. Blush is gentle, Ruby is deep and dramatic.
  • Quiet and elegant. Carrara, Pencil Sketch and Sandstone are calm and restrained, with soft backgrounds.
  • Deep and dramatic. Midnight Blue, Noir and Blueprint are dark and bold, and they look striking on a screen.
  • Natural and calm. Sage, Ocean and Blue Water bring greens and blues, and they are perfect if water is part of your story.
  • Playful. Crayon Art, Pop Art and Chalkboard Cafe are lively.
  • Artistic. Watercolor Wash, Japanese Ink, Retro Vintage, Neon and Aurora each have a strong personality.

Choose by comparing, not by name. Set up the view, click through five or six themes without changing anything else, and notice the one that makes you say "that's it". Then check three things: does it suit the room, does it suit the person, and will you still like it in a year? If two themes tie, take the calmer one.

For a first anniversary, many people like a warm or quiet theme, because it suits a bedroom or a hallway, and a deeper theme for a living room statement wall. There is no rule, and the best choice is the one that looks like the two of you.

Choosing the layout and size

The layout is the shape and size of the finished image. Maparte has 42 layouts in five groups: 18 print sizes, 10 social media shapes, 7 wallpaper layouts, 4 iPhone layouts and 3 web layouts. For a first anniversary, a few matter most.

If you plan to frame it, measure the frame before you design. A poster made at one proportion and placed in a frame of another needs cropping or a border. If you only plan to give the file, a square or phone layout is a good first choice, and you can make a second layout later.

A particularly nice idea is to give the same map in two forms: a phone wallpaper on the day and a printed poster later. The wallpaper is a gift for the morning. The poster is a gift for the wall. Because the editor keeps your settings, switching the layout is quick.

The words on the poster

Plain words often work best on a first anniversary. Two names and a date can carry more feeling than a paragraph. Here are some ideas, grouped by tone. They are examples to adapt, not text to copy.

Plain. "Anna and Rahul", "One year", "14 February 2020 to 14 February 2021".

Warm. "Year one, and counting", "Here is where it started", "Our first year, our first street".

Playful. "Still not tired of you", "One year, zero regrets about the sofa", "Twelve months of good decisions".

Paper-themed. "Year one, on paper", "The first page of many", "Chapter one".

Formal. A pair of coordinates, a month and a year. It looks like a label from a museum and it keeps the secret.

Silent. The map, a tiny date and nothing else.

A few rules keep the words working.

  • Short beats clever. If the recipient has to ask what it means, it is too clever.
  • One typeface. Maparte offers a list of typefaces, grouped as serif, sans serif, display and monospace. Pick one and use it throughout.
  • Check the date format. Day first or month first? Use the one your partner uses.
  • Mind the line length. Long lines in a narrow layout can wrap awkwardly. Look at the preview.
  • Leave space. A poster with room around the text looks more confident.

Details that make it look designed

Once the place, the theme and the layout are right, a few details give the poster a finished look.

Border. Maparte offers 13 border styles, with adjustable thickness and spacing. A thin single or double line gives a framed look. No border is cleaner for a wallpaper. If you plan to put the poster in a frame with a mat, a thin border is plenty.

Markers. The Markers panel lets you add a built-in icon, an emoji or a custom upload. One small marker on the exact spot is usually enough. For a story with several places, use a few small markers and keep them the same style.

Coordinates. A line of coordinates is a quiet, elegant detail. It looks designed, and it is a small clue that the place is special.

The data credit. Maps are drawn from OpenStreetMap contributors, and the credit appears on the poster in small print. Keep it legible and make sure that nothing overlaps it.

Wall preview. The editor has a wall photo preview that lets you see how the poster might look in a room. It is a useful sanity check before you choose a size.

Step by step in the Maparte editor

The whole process takes about fifteen to thirty minutes the first time.

  1. Open maparte.com and go to the editor. Type the place name, or the coordinates, in the location field.
  2. Frame the view. Pan and zoom until the key spot sits a little above the centre and one big shape such as a river is visible.
  3. Choose the layout. Match your frame, or start with A4 Portrait or Square Caption A4.
  4. Choose the theme. Click through five or six on the same view.
  5. Set the border. A thin line, a double line, or none.
  6. Write the text. A title, a subtitle and a line of details. Read each aloud.
  7. Add a marker. A small one, on the exact spot.
  8. Check the 4K preview. Zoom into the corners, the text and the thin lines.
  9. Download. Open the download panel and choose a format. Save the file in two places. The panel lists PNG, JPG, TIFF, a PNG and JPG pack, PDF, SVG and an all-in-one master bundle, and raster exports go up to 300 pixels per inch. Always check the live panel for the current options and terms.

If it is a surprise, do the work on your own device and keep the file in a folder with a dull name.

Which file format should I pick?

  • PNG is a safe general choice. It opens everywhere, keeps lines crisp and suits wallpapers and most prints.
  • JPG is smaller, and suits messages and sharing.
  • TIFF is a large, high-quality file that some print shops prefer.
  • PDF is handy for print shops, because the page size is part of the file.
  • SVG is a vector format. It scales to any size without losing sharpness, which helps for very large prints and for editing in design programs.
  • The master bundle collects several formats in one download, which is convenient when you do not know what the printer will want.

If you are unsure, take the PNG, and ask the print shop whether they prefer another format.

Printing it: a short guide

Maparte does not print or ship posters, so if you want a paper version, this step is yours. It is not difficult.

  • Home printer. Fine for A4 and smaller. Use good paper and the printer's best quality setting.
  • Local print shop. Best for A3 and larger, and for a wider choice of paper. Ask which format they prefer and whether they will print the file at its original size without resizing or cropping.
  • Test print. Ask for a small test on the same paper before a large run, because screens and paper show colour differently. Bright, saturated colours often look calmer on paper, and dark areas can look heavier.
  • Paper choice. Matte paper gives a quiet, modern look. Satin or semi-gloss makes colours feel deeper.
  • Frame. Choose the frame first and design to its proportions. A thin frame with a mat suits a map with fine lines.

For reference, here is how many pixels common sizes need at 300 pixels per inch. Large posters seen from a distance can use fewer, so treat the largest sizes as comfortable rather than essential.

Gifts to go with the poster

A poster is a complete gift, but a small companion can make it feel more finished. These are ideas, not requirements.

  • A handwritten card. Three or four plain sentences about the year. Handwriting adds warmth that no typeface can match.
  • A small plant. A living thing that grows over the year ahead, and a quiet echo of the idea that a relationship grows.
  • A book. Something you read together, or something you both wanted to read. Paper again.
  • A meal at the place on the map. If the poster shows a restaurant, a cafe or a park, plan the visit. The poster becomes an invitation.
  • A picnic on the spot. For a poster of a park or a viewpoint, take it with you, and hold it up in the place it shows.
  • A framed copy and a digital copy. One on the wall, one on the phone.

None of these needs to cost much. The point is attention, and a map is already a gift of attention.

Using the poster to start a tradition

A first anniversary is a good moment to start something that grows each year. Here are some ideas that keep the effort small.

One poster a year, in the same style. Each year, choose a place from the past twelve months and make a poster with the same theme, layout, typeface and text position. Over time the posters form a set, and the pattern of the maps tells your story.

A place you visit every anniversary. If the first poster is of a particular park or cafe, make it the place you return to each year. A photo taken in the same spot each year can sit beside the poster.

A box of keepsakes. Keep the card, the ticket and the receipt from each year in a box. When you have a few, the posters and the box together form a small archive.

A saved design. Write down your settings after the first poster: the theme, the layout, the typeface, the border and the position of the text. Next year, you can match them in minutes.

What if your first year was not smooth?

Some first years are hard. There may have been a move, an illness, a job loss or a long separation. A poster does not need to pretend otherwise. It can celebrate the fact that you got through it. A map of the place that sheltered you, a hospital neighbourhood, a temporary flat, a street you walked for hours, can be one of the most moving gifts of all. Use plain words, such as "We made it through", and leave the rest to the map.

A note about timing and surprises

If the poster is a surprise, there are a few practical points.

  • Work on your own device. Keep the file in a folder with a plain name.
  • Do not leave the editor open. A browser tab left open on a shared computer will give the game away.
  • Ask for details carefully. If you need a date or a place, ask a friend, or find it in your own photos.
  • Have a backup plan. If a print is late, give the file on the day and the print when it arrives. A digital file is a gift that cannot be late.

Eight first-anniversary ideas, worked through

Here are eight scenarios, with the choices that suit each. The people and places are invented to show how the decisions work. They are illustrations, not real customers.

One: the flat

A couple moved in together three months after meeting. The first-anniversary poster shows their street, close in, with a marker on their door. The theme is a warm Terracotta, which matches the brick of the building. The caption is the street name, "Our first flat", and the date they got the keys. It hangs in the hallway, where they see it every time they leave.

Two: the city they moved to

Both moved to the same city in the same year and met at a friend's party. A wide view shows the whole city, with its river and green belt. A deep Midnight Blue theme with gold lines. A tiny marker shows the district where they met. The caption is the city name and one year. It works on a living room wall, because the city pattern reads from across the room.

Three: the wedding venue

They married in a small town hall. The map shows the town hall and the park around it, in a calm, light theme, with a double border. A serif title, the date of the wedding and the date of the first anniversary. It looks like a certificate and it suits the paper tradition.

Four: the long-distance first year

One of them lives in a different country. The poster shows a wide map with two markers, one for each home, and a third on the airport where they first met in person. A cool, quiet theme. The caption is a line they both use as a joke. Because the poster is a file, they can each print a copy in their own country and hang them at the same time.

Five: the holiday

They spent a week on an island. A wide, water-forward Ocean theme, with the island in the middle and a marker on the guesthouse. Coordinates and a month in the caption. It is a quiet reminder of a good week, and it looks good in a bathroom or a bedroom.

Six: the day out

A map of the day they got engaged: the cafe, the park bench, the restaurant. Three small markers, one for each stop, and a thin line of text. The theme is Blush, soft and warm. It is a map that tells a day from morning to night.

Seven: a route they ran

They trained together and ran a first 10 km. A route poster shows the course as a bright line over a dark city. Start and finish markers, the race name, the date and their finishing time. Our guide to route posters covers the steps. Check each figure against the race confirmation before you finish.

Eight: the phone wallpaper surprise

A quick, simple gift. A tall poster of the place where they met, in the phone's exact layout, sent on the morning of the day with the line "Look at your lock screen". It takes ten minutes and a little thought. The printed version follows later.

How to give it

The way you give a poster matters nearly as much as the poster.

Send the file in the morning. A message with the image and one line of text is a lovely start to the day.

Hand over a printed poster. Roll it in a tube or lay it flat in a folder. A framed print is a heavier gift, and it is best given in person.

Put it in a card. A small A5 print folded into a card, with a handwritten line inside.

Hide it. Hang it on the wall before they get home, or tuck the file onto their desktop as a background. The surprise is the gift.

Make it a game. Hand it over and ask: "Do you know where this is?" Let them find the marker. The moment of recognition is the best part.

Make it a pair. If there are two of you, each of you can give the other a poster of a different place. You end up with a set.

A letter to go with it

A short note makes any gift more personal. You do not need to be a writer. Three or four sentences are enough: what you remember about the place, what the year has meant, and what you hope for the next one. Write it by hand, and keep it plain.

Sets, and the years to come

The first anniversary is a good time to start a tradition. One idea is a set of posters, one for each year, all in the same theme and layout. Over time they build a gallery wall, and the pattern of the maps tells the story of the couple.

To make a matching set, keep these the same: the theme, the layout, the typeface, the border and the position of the text. Change only the place and the words. Write the settings down, or keep the design saved, so that you can match it next year.

You do not need to plan the set in advance. Many people make one poster and then realise, a year later, that they want a second. Because the settings are simple, it is easy to match them.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • A story that does not fit. If the view is too wide for a street story or too close for a city story, the poster feels off. Compare both views.
  • Too many markers. Three or four are plenty.
  • Too much text. Let the map speak.
  • Mixing typefaces. One is enough.
  • A mismatch with the room. Check the wall colour.
  • Leaving it to the day. Allow time for a test print if you want paper.
  • A layout that does not fit the frame. Choose the layout to match the frame.
  • Skipping the proofread. A wrong date or a misspelt name is hard to forgive. Spell each word aloud, check each digit, and ask someone else to read it.
  • Skipping the full-size check. The 4K preview exists for a reason. Zoom into the corners and the thin lines.

A checklist before you download

  1. The place is right and the marker sits on the exact spot.
  2. The names are spelt correctly, letter by letter.
  3. Every digit in the date and the coordinates has been read twice.
  4. The date is in the format your partner uses.
  5. No text is cut off by the border or the edge.
  6. The data credit is present and legible.
  7. The layout matches the frame, or the phone.
  8. You have looked at the preview at full size.
  9. You have saved the file in two places.
  10. You have opened the downloaded file from the folder and zoomed in.

Frequently asked questions

What is the traditional gift for a first anniversary?

In many traditions the first year is linked to paper. A poster fits that idea, and so do cards, letters and books. The tradition is a charming starting point, not a rule.

Can I give only a digital file?

Yes. A file can be sent as a message or set as a wallpaper, and it can be printed later. Many people give the file on the day and a framed print a few weeks later.

Does Maparte print or ship posters?

No. Maparte provides digital files only. You download a file and use it on a screen, share it or take it to a printer of your choice.

Which file format should I choose?

PNG is a safe general choice. JPG is smaller, TIFF and PDF are popular with print shops, and SVG is a vector format for very large uses. The live download panel lists the current formats.

How big can I print it?

Raster exports go up to 300 pixels per inch, and the editor states the pixel size for each layout. Compare it with the size you want. Large posters seen from a distance can use fewer pixels per inch.

Can I use it as a phone wallpaper?

Yes. Maparte has wallpaper layouts, including four iPhone layouts and a Galaxy layout. For the iPhone 16/17 Pro and iPhone 17, the layout is 1206 x 2622 pixels.

Can I show two places on one poster?

Yes. Place a marker at each, and choose a view that includes both. For places that are far apart, such as two countries, a wide view works best.

Can I add a route?

Yes. The editor accepts GPX files under 10 MB. If you do not have a track file, markers for the start and the finish tell the story too. A route is a good fit if you did something together such as a run or a long walk.

Are the maps accurate?

The map data comes from OpenStreetMap contributors and is detailed in most towns and cities. The poster is a work of art and is not meant for navigation.

Can I sell or resell the posters?

No. Maparte's terms allow personal, non-commercial use. Check the live terms for the current position.

What if I do not have much time?

A wallpaper takes about ten minutes. A poster takes about thirty. A printed version needs more time, so give yourself a week or two.

Final thoughts

A first-anniversary map gift is small and exact. It takes one place from your year and gives it a shape that you can hang on a wall or carry in your pocket. Choose the story, frame it so it is readable, pick a theme that matches the mood, write only words you have checked and look at the preview at full size.

When you are ready, open the Maparte editor at maparte.com, type in the place and try three themes. If your story is about where you met, read our guide to the where-we-met map. The best first-anniversary gift is not the fanciest. It is the one that says, without any fuss, "I remember".
