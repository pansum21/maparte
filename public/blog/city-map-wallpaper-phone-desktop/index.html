A wallpaper is the picture you see most. You unlock your phone dozens of times a day, and you open your laptop at the same desk every morning. A map of a place that matters to you, a home town, the city you studied in, the street where you met someone, turns that picture into a small daily reminder. This guide explains how to make a city map wallpaper for an iPhone, an Android phone, an iPad, an e-ink reader or a desktop monitor: which size to choose, how to frame the map, which themes work best behind icons, and how to set the result as your background.

We make Maparte, a browser tool for map posters and wallpapers. It is digital only: you design in the browser, check a 4K preview and download a file. We do not print or ship anything. A wallpaper is the most natural use of that, because a wallpaper has no paper to ship.

The short version. Pick the layout that matches your device exactly. Choose a calm theme with good contrast. Frame a quiet area where the clock and icons will sit. Skip the border and long text. Download the file and set it as your background.

Why a map makes a good wallpaper

Most wallpapers are photographs, and photographs are busy. A map is a pattern of lines and shapes that stays interesting without competing with your icons. Here is why maps work in particular.

  • They are personal. A photograph of a famous place says "I like this". A map of your own place says "this is mine".
  • They are calm. Roads and rivers form a quiet texture. With a dark or muted theme the background recedes and icons stay readable.
  • They suit dark mode. Several themes, such as Midnight Blue, Noir and Aurora, are dark by design and look right behind a dark interface.
  • They scale well. Because the map is drawn from vector-style data, the lines stay crisp at the size of your screen.
  • They are different. A map wallpaper is not the same as the default pictures every phone ships with.

How to find your model

On an iPhone, open Settings, then General, then About, and read the model name. Match it against the list above. On Android, open Settings and look for "About phone". If your model is not on the list, choose the closest tall layout. An Android phone that is not a Galaxy S24 Ultra will usually have a similar tall screen, and the system will scale and crop the wallpaper to fit. Since the shape is close, little is lost. If you want an exact fit for another model, tell us which one, and we can note it as a request.

Why exact size matters

If a wallpaper is made for a different screen than the one it ends up on, the phone has to scale or crop it, and what it crops is often what you cared about. A wallpaper made at the right size lines up with the lock screen as intended, so the clock and widgets fall where you planned. This matters more for a map than for a photograph, because a map's subject is usually its centre and its structure.

Framing the map for a phone

A phone wallpaper has two jobs that pull in different directions. It needs to look good as a picture, and it needs to leave room for the clock, the notifications and the icons. Here is how to handle both.

Think in zones

Picture the screen divided into three bands. The top band holds the time and the date on a lock screen. The middle band holds your main content. The bottom band holds the dock and, on some phones, shortcut buttons. A good map wallpaper places its interesting feature, such as a river bend, in the middle band, and keeps the top and bottom calmer.

Use a tall subject

Phones are tall, so tall subjects fit well: a long river, an avenue, a peninsula, a coastline that runs north to south. A wide city can still work, but it will be cropped by the shape, so zoom in on the part you want.

Keep the text minimal

A wallpaper rarely needs a title. If you want one, a short line in the lower third, away from the clock, is enough. Many people prefer no text at all, because the map alone is the picture.

Skip the border

A border on a phone screen wastes space and can look like a frame around a frame. Choose the clean full-bleed option.

The example above uses the Terracotta theme on Tokyo. The soft clay lines on cream stay in the background, and the quiet water at the bottom leaves room for icons, which is exactly what a wallpaper needs.

Themes that work behind icons

Not every theme suits a wallpaper. A theme is good for a wallpaper when its roads are visible but not harsh, and when it has no large patches of very bright colour that fight with icons.

Dark and calm

Midnight Blue, Noir, Aurora and Blueprint are dark or deep. They suit dark-mode phones, and white icons and text stand out clearly. Noir is the most dramatic, with white lines on black. Midnight Blue is softer, with warm lines on deep navy. Aurora is atmospheric, with cool colours and a glow.

Soft and light

Sage, Sandstone, Blush, Carrara and Terracotta are gentle. They suit light-mode phones and give a calm, paper-like look. If your icons are dark, a pale theme helps them stay legible.

Artistic

Pencil Sketch, Watercolor Wash, Crayon Art and Japanese Ink have a hand-made character. They look lovely, but their textures can be busier behind icons. Use them on a lock screen, where there are fewer icons, or choose a quiet part of the map.

Bold

Neon, Ruby, Sunset and Pop Art are strong. They make a striking lock screen, though their brightness can compete with a crowded home screen. Try them on a lock screen first.

A simple way to decide is to place your phone's current home screen next to the preview and imagine the icons on top. If the icons would be hard to read, choose a calmer theme.

Setting it as the wallpaper

On an iPhone, open the picture in Photos, choose the share button, and look for the option to use it as a wallpaper. Or go to Settings, then Wallpaper, then add a new one, and pick the photo. You can set it for the lock screen, the home screen or both. The exact menu names change between versions of the system, so look for "Wallpaper" in Settings if the steps differ on your phone.

Android phones

Maparte has one Android-specific layout, the Galaxy S24 Ultra at 1440 by 3120 pixels. That is a very tall screen, and the shape is common among modern large Android phones. If you use a different Android phone, the nearest tall layout is the best choice. Here is how to decide.

  1. Find your screen resolution. Search for your model name and "screen resolution", or look at the specifications page of the maker.
  2. Compare the shape. Divide the height by the width. The Galaxy S24 Ultra layout is about 2.17 tall for every 1 wide. If your phone is similar, the layout will fit well.
  3. If yours is shorter, such as 2:1 or 16:9, some of the top and bottom of the wallpaper will be cropped. Frame the map so the subject is in the centre, and leave the top and bottom plain.
  4. Preview in the editor, then on the phone. Set the wallpaper, look at the home screen and the lock screen, and adjust the framing if the important part is hidden.

Setting it

On most Android phones, open the picture in the gallery, choose the menu, and look for "Set as wallpaper" or "Use as". Or open Settings, then Wallpaper, and choose from the gallery. Many phones let you drag the picture to adjust the crop before you confirm. Menu names vary by maker, so look for "Wallpaper" in Settings.

iPad and tablets

Tablets are used in both orientations, and their screens are less tall than phones. Maparte offers the portrait shapes: 1668 by 2388 pixels for the 11-inch iPad Pro and 2048 by 2732 pixels for the 12.9-inch model. Both are close to a 3:4 ratio, which is much squarer than a phone. That is good news for maps, because a 3:4 canvas shows a generous block of city in both directions, so it looks like a proper map and not a narrow strip.

Because a tablet may rotate, a map is a good subject. A map has no obvious "up" other than the title, and a pattern of streets looks natural in either orientation. If you add text, test the layout in the orientation you use most.

Desktop and laptop wallpapers

A desktop wallpaper is wide. Maparte offers three: Full HD at 1920 by 1080, 4K at 3840 by 2160, and Ultrawide at 3440 by 1440.

Choosing between them

  • Full HD suits most laptops and standard monitors.
  • 4K suits high-resolution monitors, where a smaller image would look soft.
  • Ultrawide suits 21:9 style monitors.

If you are not sure, check your display settings for the resolution, and choose the layout with the same ratio. If your screen has a different ratio, such as 16:10 on some laptops, the nearest 16:9 layout will be cropped a little at the top and bottom or at the sides.

Framing for a desktop

A desktop wallpaper is crowded by icons on the left, a taskbar or dock at the bottom or the top, and windows in the middle. Here are some pointers.

  • Put the subject off-centre. Icons usually sit on the left, so a subject slightly to the right is clearer.
  • Choose a calm theme. A busy map behind many icons is tiring to look at.
  • Think about the taskbar. Leave the bottom edge quiet.
  • Use full bleed. A border wastes space on a monitor.
  • Consider a dark theme. A dark wallpaper is easier on the eyes in a dim room.

The ultrawide

The ultrawide canvas turns the map into a band. It works best for places with a long horizontal feature, such as a river, a coastline or a skyline of streets along a waterfront.

Setting a desktop wallpaper

On Windows, right-click the image file and choose the option to set it as the desktop background, or open Settings, then Personalisation, then Background. On a Mac, open System Settings, then Wallpaper, and add the image. On Linux, the settings differ by desktop, but most have a "Set as wallpaper" option in the file manager. In each case, choose the fit option, which is usually called "Fill" or "Fit". Use "Fill" if the image matches your screen ratio.

E-ink readers

Maparte includes a layout for the Xteink X4, an e-ink reader with a 480 by 800 pixel screen. E-ink screens show greys, not colour, and update slowly, which changes the design.

  • Choose high contrast. Noir, Carrara or Pencil Sketch tend to look crisp on e-ink.
  • Avoid fine detail. At 480 by 800 pixels, very thin lines can disappear. Zoom in to a simpler area.
  • Skip subtle colours. Pale colours turn into similar greys. Dark roads on a light ground, or the reverse, are clearest.
  • Keep the text bold. A short title in a strong typeface reads best.

This layout is a niche feature, and if you do not own that device, you can ignore it.

Lock screen or home screen?

You can use the same map on both screens, or different ones. Here are three ideas.

  1. The same map on both. Simple and consistent. The map continues behind your icons.
  2. A detailed map on the lock screen and a calmer version on the home screen. For example, the same place in a lighter theme.
  3. Two places. The lock screen shows where you live, the home screen shows where you are from.

Because a digital file is easy to copy, you can try several combinations and change them when you like.

Seasonal and occasion ideas for a map wallpaper

A wallpaper does not have to stay the same for a year. Because a new layout is only a few minutes of work, you can change it with the season, and each change can carry a small meaning.

  • A new-year map. On 1 January, set a map of a place you want to visit this year. It works as a quiet reminder of a plan.
  • A monsoon or winter palette. Cool blues and greys such as Ocean, Blue Water or Blueprint suit rainy and cold months. Warm palettes such as Sandstone, Sunset and Terracotta suit summer.
  • A travel countdown. Before a trip, put the destination on your lock screen. Each unlock becomes a small bit of anticipation.
  • A birthday or anniversary surprise. Make a wallpaper of the place where something began and send it to the person as a digital gift. It is quick to share, and it is more personal than a stock photo.
  • A home-town map for a student far away. If you or a family member lives in another city, a map of the home town on the phone is a gentle companion.
  • A race-day wallpaper. Upload your route as a GPX file, and keep the finished map on your phone during training, so the course becomes familiar.
  • A shared wallpaper for a couple. Choose the place where you met and set the same map on both phones, in two different themes if your tastes differ.

None of these needs special skills. They need a place, a theme and about ten minutes.

Lock screen versus home screen, in more detail

The lock screen and the home screen ask different things from a picture, and it is worth designing them separately.

The lock screen shows a clock and often a few widgets. The picture is seen for a moment each time, so it can be bolder, more graphic and more personal. A dramatic dark theme with one clear shape, such as a coastline or a river bend, works well here. Leave the top third calm, because the clock sits there.

The home screen carries rows of icons and folders. The picture is seen for a long time, so it needs to be quieter. Choose a muted palette, zoom into an area with plenty of space, and avoid strong contrast in the middle of the screen. A map with a very fine network of streets can compete with app names, so a calmer part of the map is often better.

If your phone lets you set two different pictures, make two layouts of the same place. Use a bold version for the lock screen and a calm version for the home screen. They will feel related without fighting for attention.

Working with your phone's dark mode

Many phones switch between light and dark appearance, and some can change the wallpaper's brightness along with them. A few points help.

  • Test in both modes. Put the wallpaper on your phone and switch the appearance. Icon labels change colour, and a label that was clear on a dark picture may be hard to read on a pale one.
  • Pick a theme that handles both. Mid-tone palettes such as Sage or Sandstone are usually more forgiving than a very bright or very dark one.
  • Make a pair. If you like both appearances, make a light version and a dark version of the same place and switch when you want.
  • Check the widgets. Widgets add their own panels, and a busy map behind them can be distracting.

Small details that make a wallpaper feel finished

After the main choices, a few small details separate a quick experiment from a wallpaper you enjoy for months.

  1. Check the spelling of the place name. If you add a title, read it twice. Local spellings and accents matter.
  2. Choose one typeface. Maparte offers a list to choose from. Use one for the title and, if you add a second line, keep it small and quiet.
  3. Use a marker sparingly. A single marker on a meaningful spot is stronger than many.
  4. Keep the margins. A wallpaper is cropped differently by different phones, so keep important elements away from the edges.
  5. Look at it at arm's length. A phone is held at a distance. If the picture only works when you lean in, simplify it.
  6. Try one quiet border or none. A border is a feature of a print. On a screen, a full-bleed layout usually looks cleaner.

A short worked example

To make this concrete, here is how a wallpaper could be made from nothing, in a few minutes. The place and the choices are an example, not a recommendation.

You want a wallpaper for a recent iPhone, with a map of the town where you grew up. You open maparte.com and search for the town. You zoom until the river and the main road are both visible, and the old market sits slightly below the centre. You choose the iPhone layout that matches your model. The editor shows the tall shape of the screen, and you notice that the top fifth will sit behind the clock, so you drag the map a little to leave the top calm.

You try three themes. Noir is dramatic but too dark for your taste. Sage is calm, and the river stands out in a pale line. Sandstone is warm and a little busy. You keep Sage. You add a small marker on the market, and a short title in the lower third with the name of the town, using a plain typeface. You look at the 4K preview, zoom in to check the spelling, and download the PNG.

You send the file to your phone as a document, so it is not compressed, open it, and set it as the wallpaper. You switch to dark mode and check the icons. Then you make a second version for your lock screen with a bolder theme, because you like the idea of a contrast. The whole thing took about as long as a cup of tea.

Before you set it: a short checklist

A wallpaper is easy to change, but a few checks save you from setting one, hating it and going back to start.

  1. Is the layout the one for your device? Check the model name in the layout list, and the pixel size shown in the panel.
  2. Is the clock area calm? Imagine the time sitting over the top third of the picture.
  3. Can you read your icons? Look at the preview with a pretend grid of icons in your mind, especially in the middle band.
  4. Does the title help? If it adds nothing, remove it.
  5. Did you pick PNG? For crisp thin lines, it is the safer choice.
  6. Did the file arrive at full size? After sending it to your phone, open the details and confirm the pixel size, if your phone shows it.
  7. Have you looked at it in the sun? A wallpaper that looks good indoors can wash out in bright light, and a darker theme is more forgiving outdoors.

If every answer is yes, set it and live with it for a few days before you judge it. A good wallpaper grows on you, and if it does not, you can make another in ten minutes.

PNG or JPG for a wallpaper?

The download panel lists seven options: PNG, JPG, TIFF, a PNG and JPG pack, PDF, SVG and a master bundle. For a wallpaper only the first two matter. For a wallpaper, the choice is simple.

  • PNG keeps every line sharp with no compression artefacts. It is the best choice for a map, which is made of thin lines and flat colours. The files are larger.
  • JPG is smaller, which is handy for sending by message. At good quality it looks fine, though very fine lines can show slight softness when you zoom in.
  • TIFF is mostly for printing, and you will not need it for a wallpaper.

If you are not sure, take the PNG for your own device and a JPG to share. The panel states the pixel size for the layout you chose, so you can confirm it matches your screen.

Sending the file to your phone

You made the wallpaper on a computer, and you want it on your phone. There are several ways.

  1. Open the editor on the phone itself. Download the file in the phone's browser, and it lands in your downloads or photos.
  2. Send it to yourself in a message or by email. Open it on the phone and save it to your photos.
  3. Use a cloud folder. Save it on the computer, and open the folder on the phone.
  4. Use a cable or a nearby-share feature. Copy the file across.

Whichever you use, check that the phone does not compress the image on the way. Some messaging apps shrink pictures. If the wallpaper looks soft, send the file as a document or through a cloud folder, so it arrives at full size.

Think about privacy before you map your home

A map of your own street as a wallpaper is a lovely idea, and it is also a picture of where you live. If someone sees your phone, they may see that location. If you share the wallpaper on social media, the same applies. Here are some sensible habits.

  • Frame the neighbourhood, not the front door. A wider view of your area is less exact than a close view of your own house.
  • Do not add your full address as text. A district or a city name is enough.
  • Think before you share. A wallpaper you post publicly shows the place to everyone who sees it.
  • Use a different place if you prefer. A map of your home town, or a favourite city, tells the story without pinpointing your current home.

This is not a reason to avoid map wallpapers. It is a small piece of care.

Making a matching set for your devices

One of the pleasures of a digital tool is that one idea becomes a family of files. If you own a phone, a tablet and a desktop, you can make a matching set.

  1. Choose one place and one theme. Consistency makes the set feel designed.
  2. Make each layout in turn. The map will be reframed for each shape, so check each preview.
  3. Adjust the framing per device. A phone needs the subject in the middle band. A desktop needs it off-centre. The tablet can show a larger area.
  4. Keep the text the same, or remove it. A small title in the same typeface on each device ties them together.
  5. Name the files clearly. For example, "lisbon-iphone-ocean.png", "lisbon-desktop-ocean.png".

If you share a computer or a tablet with family, you could give each person their own place, such as a map of each person's home town.

Choosing places that look good as wallpapers

Some places make better wallpapers than others. Here are the kinds that tend to work.

  • Places with water. A river, a harbour or a coast gives a strong shape and a calm colour.
  • Places with a clear structure. A grid, a radial pattern or a ring road gives a pleasing texture.
  • Planned cities and old centres. Both are beautiful in different ways, one calm and the other tangled.
  • Islands and peninsulas. The silhouette alone makes a picture.
  • Small towns. Few roads mean a lot of calm space, which is ideal behind icons.

Places that need more care include very large regions, where the map becomes a fine web of lines, and places with little mapped detail, where the picture may look empty. In both cases, zoom in to the part you love.

Common problems and fixes

  • The wallpaper looks blurry. Check that you chose the layout for your device and downloaded the PNG. Check that a messaging app did not compress the file.
  • The important part is hidden by the clock. Reframe the map so the subject sits lower, or leave the top calm.
  • The icons are hard to read. Choose a calmer or darker theme, or a quiet part of the map.
  • The wallpaper is cropped. The device ratio differs from the layout. Choose the closest layout, or frame the subject centrally so cropping affects only the edges.
  • The colours look different on the phone. Phone screens vary in brightness and colour. Compare with your usual wallpaper and adjust the theme if needed.
  • The desktop wallpaper is not sharp. Choose the 4K layout if your monitor is 4K.
  • The map is too busy. Zoom in and use a calm theme.

Can I print a wallpaper?

A wallpaper layout is sized for a screen, which is a small number of pixels compared with a large print. You can print it at a small size, such as a postcard, but for a poster choose a print layout such as A4 Portrait or Poster 18 x 24 in, which has a higher pixel count for paper. Our guide to making a city map poster covers printing in more detail. Maparte does not print, so you would take the file to a print shop or print it yourself.
