One city, twenty-one themes. How each palette behaves, which moods it suits, and how to choose without second-guessing.

Ask ten people what makes a map poster beautiful and most of them will talk about the city. The streets, the river, the shape of the coast. Those matter, but they are the same for everyone who picks the same place. What makes your version feel like yours is the theme: the set of colours that turns raw street data into a mood. The same Paris can feel like a luxury atlas, a child's drawing, a midnight neon sign or an old parchment chart, and nothing about the geography has changed.

Maparte's editor ships with a library of themes, and the Paris posters in this guide were made from exactly that library, one for each theme, so you can see how a single city behaves under twenty-one different palettes. This article is a practical tour. It explains what a theme actually controls, how to think about mood and contrast, what each of the twenty-one themes feels like and where it tends to shine, and how to match a theme to a place, a room, a person or a screen.

A quick note on how to read this guide. We describe each theme using the real colours defined in the editor, quoted as hex codes where that helps, plus what the finished poster looks like in the captures you can see on the page. Where two themes look similar, we say so plainly. And because Maparte is a browser tool with a 4K preview, you do not have to trust any description. Open the editor, put in your city, and click through the themes yourself. It takes seconds, and no description beats seeing your own streets in a new colour.

What a theme actually controls

A map poster looks like one image, but it is built from layers, and a theme is simply a colour assignment for each layer. Understanding the layers helps you predict how a theme will behave on a given city.

  • Land. The background colour of the whole poster. This is the biggest single decision, because it covers most of the canvas. It also sets the mood most strongly: cream says calm, navy says luxurious, black says dramatic.
  • Land cover and parks. Subtle shifts on the background that suggest green spaces and open areas. In many themes these are a half-step away from the land colour so they whisper rather than shout. In a few themes, such as Crayon Art and Retro Vintage, parks are given a proper green.
  • Water. Rivers, lakes, harbours and the sea. Water is often the most dramatic variable between themes. In Blue Water it is a vivid electric cyan; in Noir it is darker than the land; in Carrara it is a cool grey.
  • Buildings. Where buildings are drawn, they get their own tone. In Ocean they are a blue; in Neon they are magenta.
  • Rail and aeroways. Railway lines and airport areas, usually drawn in a subtle or contrasting colour.
  • Roads, in tiers. The editor gives roads several levels: major roads, then three grades of minor roads, plus paths. A theme assigns a colour to each. Major roads are usually the strongest, and the minor tiers fade progressively, which gives the street network its depth. The way a theme graduates these tiers is the difference between a flat poster and a rich one.

Add to that the text colour for the city name, country and coordinates, which is always chosen to sit comfortably on the background, and you have a complete theme.

Why the road tiers matter so much

If you take one practical idea from this section, make it this one: roads carry most of the visual weight in a city poster. In a dense city like Paris, thousands of street segments cover the canvas. If every street were the same strong colour, the result would be a tangle. By fading the minor tiers toward the background, a theme lets the major roads, the ring roads, boulevards and rivers, stand forward while the small streets form a fine texture behind them.

This is why you will see themes with very different feelings share the same underlying structure. Midnight Blue uses a bright gold for major roads and then steps down through dimmer ambers. Noir uses pure white for major roads and steps down through greys. Terracotta moves from a deep sienna to pale clay. The structure is the same; the palette is different.

When you preview a theme on your own city, look at how the major roads read compared with the small streets. If the contrast is too flat, the city looks like a grid of equal lines. If the contrast is too strong, the main roads can overpower the rest. The best themes for your place are the ones where the structure you care about is clear.

Mood and colour: a short, practical primer

You do not need to study colour theory to choose a good theme, but a few ideas are useful.

Warm and cool. Warm colours, such as orange, terracotta, gold and rose, tend to feel friendly, nostalgic and cosy. Cool colours, such as blue, teal and silver, tend to feel calm, modern and precise. This is a general tendency rather than a rule, but it is a reliable starting point. A map of a hometown for a parent might lean warm; a map of the city where you started a company might lean cool.

Light and dark. A light background feels airy and traditional, like paper. A dark background feels dramatic and contemporary, like a screen or a gallery wall in a dim room. Dark posters make coloured lines glow, which is why Neon, Ruby and Aurora look so striking. Light posters make fine lines readable and feel at home in bright rooms.

Contrast. Contrast is the difference between the lines and the background. High contrast, like white on black or black on white, reads clearly from a distance. Low contrast, like sage green on pale green, reads as a soft texture up close and nearly disappears from across a room. Neither is wrong. They suit different jobs.

Saturation. Saturation is how intense a colour is. Crayon Art and Pop Art are highly saturated and playful. Sage, Blush and Sandstone are muted and calm. A saturated theme draws attention; a muted theme lets the viewer come to it.

Restraint. The most common mistake is choosing the loudest theme for a poster that will hang for years. A bold design is thrilling for a week and can feel tiring after a year. If the poster is for a living space, favour themes with a clear, calm structure and let the map supply the interest.

The twenty-one themes, one by one

Each entry below shows the Paris poster made with that theme, the colours that define it and a plain-language sense of where it works well and where to be careful. Paris is a good test city because it has a strong ring road, a river that curves through the middle, a dense street network and a recognisable radial pattern, so you can see how every layer is handled.

1. Midnight Blue

The editor describes Midnight Blue as a deep navy background with gold and copper roads, a luxury atlas aesthetic. The land is a very dark navy (#0A1628), the water is darker still (#050B14), and the major roads are a rich gold (#D4A338) that steps down through amber to a dim bronze. The text matches, in a warm gold (#E5B84B).

This is a classic for good reason. The colour pairing, gold on navy, is long associated with old atlases, ship's charts and embossed book covers, and it makes a city feel important. It is a strong choice for an anniversary, a wedding venue, a graduation city or a place that carries real weight in someone's life. It also works well as a phone wallpaper, since dark backgrounds are easy on the eyes and the gold lines stay legible behind icons.

Watch out for places with very sparse roads. Gold on navy is most beautiful when there is a rich network to catch the light, so a tiny village may look a little empty.

2. Carrara

Carrara is named for the white marble of Tuscany, and the editor describes it as pale stone with soft charcoal veining. The background is a warm off-white (#F4F1EA), the major roads are a deep charcoal (#3A3631), the water is a quiet grey-blue (#C7CDD0), and the minor roads step through softer greys and warm taupes.

The effect is quiet and gallery-like. There is no colour to compete with the lines, so the street pattern becomes the whole subject. This is the theme to choose when you want the map to read as architecture or graphic art rather than as a map, and it sits easily in almost any interior, from a white Scandinavian room to a rustic one.

One honest caution: Carrara and Pencil Sketch, which we cover next, are very close neighbours. Both sit on a warm off-white and use charcoal lines, and on a first look they can seem almost identical. If you are choosing between them, put them side by side in the preview with your own city. The difference is subtle, and some people will prefer one and some the other.

3. Noir

Noir is the dark counterpart to Carrara. The editor calls it an obsidian dark background with silver and white roads, a modern gallery aesthetic. The land is nearly black (#0D0E11), and the major roads are pure white, with the minor tiers dropping through silver to a muted grey.

It is bold and graphic, and the greatest strength of Noir is how clear the structure is. White on black has the highest contrast of any combination, so even a very detailed city reads cleanly. It is a good theme for big cities, for cities you want to look serious or cinematic, and for anyone who likes a monochrome look. The Mumbai A4 card from the layouts guide uses Noir.

Think about where it will be seen. On a screen, Noir looks superb. On a wall, a black poster can be quite heavy, so it suits a large wall or a room with a strong character, and less well a small, bright one.

4. Blue Water

Blue Water is described as a high-contrast studio style: a pristine silver canvas with vibrant electric cyan water. The land is a light grey (#F2F2F2), the roads are a cool slate (#2D3748 stepping down to pale blue-grey), and the water is a saturated cyan (#46BCEC).

It is the theme to choose when water is the hero. A river, a bay, a harbour or a lake becomes a bright ribbon through an otherwise restrained map, and the eye goes straight to it. For Paris, the Seine becomes the star. For Venice, Sydney, Lisbon, Hong Kong or Mumbai, with their dramatic relationships to the water, it can be spectacular.

For an inland city with no significant water, there will be little cyan to see and the theme becomes a simple grey-on-white design. In that case, other themes will give you more character.

5. Pencil Sketch

Pencil Sketch is described as hand-drawn graphite strokes on textured ivory sketchbook paper. The background is a warm ivory (#F5F2EB), the major roads are a near-black graphite (#262626), and the minor roads soften through mid-grey. The water is a pale greige.

It reads as a drawing rather than a printed map. If you like a hand-made feel, this is the way to get it, and it is a natural fit for architects, designers, teachers and anyone for whom drawing is part of life. It is also a safe choice for people who do not want any colour, because graphite on ivory goes with everything.

As noted above, it is similar to Carrara. In our captures, Pencil Sketch looks very slightly darker and crisper in the line work, while Carrara looks a touch softer. If you want to be sure, try both.

6. Crayon Art

Crayon Art is the first of the playful themes: vibrant wax crayon colours on drawing paper. The land is a warm white (#FFFDF7), the major roads are a bright red (#FF4757), the minor roads are orange, then yellow, then lavender, and the water is a sky blue (#38B6FF). Parks are given a fresh green.

It is cheerful, it is bright, and it looks like something a child might make with a very good set of crayons. That makes it a wonderful choice for a child's room, a family map, a map of a school or a playground, or a map of a place that stands for childhood and fun. It can also be a delightful gift for someone who does not take themselves too seriously.

It is less suited to a formal setting. And because it uses so many colours, it works best when the city is simple enough that the colours can breathe. A very dense city may look busy.

7. Pop Art

Pop Art is described as a bold vintage comic book aesthetic with high-voltage ink outlines. The land is a warm cream (#FFF8E7), the major roads are solid black (#111111), the secondary roads are a punchy red (#E63946), the third tier is orange and the fourth a teal, and the water is a vivid cyan (#00B4D8).

The black and red combination makes the poster feel loud in a good way. The ring road and main avenues stand out like inked outlines in a comic panel, and the minor streets fill in with red. It is retro, graphic and confident, and it makes a strong statement in a room that wants personality: a studio, a teenager's bedroom, a games room or a cafe.

Because it is bold, give it space. A single Pop Art poster on a plain wall is striking. Several of them with other loud pieces can be too much.

8. Watercolor Wash

Watercolor Wash is described as delicate watercolour pigments bleeding softly on cold-press paper. The land is a soft cream (#FAF7F2), the major roads are a muted navy blue (#3D5A80), and the minor tiers fade through pale blues. Water is a pale aqua (#8EC5D6) and parks are a gentle green.

It is the gentlest of the colourful themes. The fading of the lines toward the edges gives the poster a soft, painted feel, and the restrained blues keep it from feeling busy. It is lovely for a nursery, a bedroom, a baby announcement, a wedding gift or any space where you want calm and tenderness. It is also a good choice when the person you are giving it to likes soft colours but not pink.

Be careful with very small displays. The delicate low-contrast lines are best seen at a decent size, and on a very small screen they can lose definition.

9. Chalkboard Cafe

Chalkboard Cafe is described as a handmade schoolroom chalkboard with dusty white and vibrant pastel chalks. The land is a dark slate (#1E252B), the major roads are chalk white, and the lesser roads are drawn in yellow (#FFE066), sky blue (#74C0FC), lavender (#B197FC) and mint (#63E6BE).

It is a dark theme with a friendly personality. Where Noir is austere, Chalkboard is welcoming, and the pastel lines give a warm, handmade, cafe-menu feel. It is a good match for kitchens, cafes, studios, classrooms and anywhere with a creative and informal atmosphere. It is also an appealing gift for a teacher.

It works best when you let the colour variety do its job. Zoom in on a city with a nice mix of road types, and the pastels create a lively rhythm.

10. Sage

Sage is described as muted botanical tones with cool green roads and gentle contrast. The land is a pale sage (#DDE8DD), the major roads are a deep forest green (#3F624F), and the lines step down through lighter greens. The water is a soft grey-green (#C5D4CB) and buildings, where shown, are a mid sage (#8BAD9B).

It is calm and natural, and it makes an excellent wallpaper because it never shouts. It is also the theme that brings the Dehradun phone wallpaper from the layouts guide to life, where the mountain town's few roads sit lightly on the green. Sage is a good choice for hill stations, forest towns, university campuses, gardens and for anyone who loves plants and quiet rooms.

Its low contrast is both its charm and its limit. From across a large room, it fades into a soft green wash, which can be exactly what you want for a bedroom, but is less punchy than higher-contrast themes.

11. Terracotta

Terracotta is described as Mediterranean warmth: burnt orange and clay tones on cream. The land is a warm cream (#F5EDE4), the major roads are a rich sienna (#A0522D), and the minor tiers move through copper to a pale peach-clay. The water is a muted blue-grey (#A8C4C4), which sits beautifully against the warm roads.

It is warm without being loud, and it fits well with wood, plants, linen and natural materials. It is also a close relative of the Maparte brand palette, and it is the theme behind the Paris Instagram Square card in the layouts guide. It is a good choice for a home, a first apartment, a gift for parents or anyone who loves a warm, lived-in room.

The pale blue-grey water is a lovely detail, so choose a city with a river or a coast and let the contrast show.

12. Sandstone

Sandstone is the plainest of the warm themes, described as warm beige with muted brown lines. The land is a sand beige (#F1E9DB), the major roads are a medium brown (#8A6E45), and the water is a slightly deeper tan (#CDBC9C), so even the river is part of the same family.

It is earthy and understated, a theme for people who like neutral rooms. Because the whole palette is one colour family, it has a calm, harmonious look, and it makes the city feel old and settled, like a survey map from a desert region. It is a good match for historic places, old towns, desert and dry-climate cities, and for rooms with natural stone and wood.

The flip side is that there is little contrast between water and land, so a place defined by its river or coast may lose some of its identity. For those, Terracotta or Ocean will give more definition.

13. Blueprint

Blueprint is the classic architectural drawing look: the editor describes a technical drawing aesthetic. The land is a deep blue (#162F4D), the major roads are pure white, and the minor tiers descend through pale sky blue to a mid blue. Text is a very light blue-white (#E8F4FF).

It looks like a plan drawn by an engineer, and it is naturally right for architects, engineers, builders, urban planners, surveyors and anyone who loves the language of technical drawing. It is also a handsome pick for a new home, since a blueprint is, after all, how a house begins. The Mumbai 24 × 36 inch card in the layouts guide uses Blueprint.

Blueprint is a close visual relative of Midnight Blue. The difference is that Midnight Blue is nearly black with a gold accent and Blueprint is a clearer, mid-toned blue with white and pale-blue lines. Choose Midnight Blue for luxury and Blueprint for precision.

14. Ruby

Ruby is described as a garnet-inspired palette with rich reds over deep plum city blocks. The land is a very dark wine (#1A070F), the major roads are a vivid ruby red (#FF2B5E) with deeper reds for the minor tiers, and the text is a pale peach (#F6D7BC).

It is dramatic and romantic. Red on dark is the colour combination of velvet, candlelight and evening wear, and it makes a map feel intense and personal. It is made for anniversaries, proposals, Valentine's gifts, the city where two people met, or any place with passion attached to it.

It is also a strong theme, so it benefits from a restrained surrounding. A frame in black or dark wood and a quiet wall make it look deliberate.

15. Sunset

Sunset is described as warm oranges and pinks on soft peach, a dreamy golden hour aesthetic. The land is a soft peach-white (#FDF5F0), the major roads are a deep coral-rust (#B84830), and the minor tiers fade through salmon to a faint pink. Water is a pale blush-tan.

It is gentle and glowing, a theme that feels like the last hour of daylight. It suits romantic gifts, honeymoon destinations, beach towns, and any place you remember in the evening light. Compared with Ruby, it is the soft, optimistic version of romance.

Because the palette is pale and warm, it is a good fit for rooms with lots of natural light. In a very dark room it will read as soft and faded.

16. Blush

Blush is described as dusty rose on warm white. The land is a barely pink white (#F9F1F0), the major roads are a muted rose (#9C5566), and the minor tiers soften to pale pinks. Water is a slightly deeper dusty pink and the text is a deep burgundy (#4D1B28).

It is soft, feminine and refined, though it is not saccharine, because the rose is dusty rather than bright. It is a lovely theme for a bedroom, a nursery, a bridal gift, a mother's day present or a map of a place associated with a woman who is important to you.

Like Sage, it is low in contrast, which makes it soothing but means the lines recede at a distance.

17. Ocean

Ocean is described as various blues and teals, perfect for coastal cities. The land is a very pale blue-white (#F0F8FA), the major roads are a deep teal (#14536A), the lesser roads step through mid blue to pale aqua, and the water is a clear light blue (#B8D8E8). Buildings, where they appear, are a medium blue.

It is clean, fresh and breezy. Think of a wide light-blue river or bay set against deep teal roads. If your place has a coast, a harbour or an estuary, Ocean makes the water feel like part of the story, with blue on blue.

It also suits bathrooms, home offices, coastal homes and anyone who finds blue calming. It is lighter and more relaxed than Midnight Blue or Blueprint.

18. Neon

Neon is described as a neon-drenched, high-contrast palette with magenta and cyan accents. The land is a near-black navy (#0B0F1A), the major roads are hot magenta (#FF2D95), the secondary roads are a lighter pink and the third tier is violet (#8A33FF) fading to deep purple. The text and rail lines are electric cyan (#00F5FF).

It is the loudest of the dark themes, and it is built for the screen. On a monitor or a phone it glows. It is a natural fit for gaming setups, nightlife cities, creative studios, music fans and anyone who likes a cyberpunk aesthetic. It also works for cities famous for their lights, such as Tokyo, Las Vegas or Hong Kong.

On a wall in a quiet room, it can feel intense. Use it where intensity is the goal.

19. Japanese Ink

Japanese Ink is described as traditional ink wash inspired, minimalist with a subtle red accent. The land is a warm white (#FAF8F5), the minor streets are shades of grey, and the major roads are a deep, brick-like red (#8B2500). The result is a mostly grey-and-white poster in which the main arteries stand out in a single colour, like a vermilion seal on an ink painting.

It is restrained and elegant. The single accent colour draws the eye to the main structure of the city, and everything else recedes. It is well suited to minimalist interiors, to places in Japan or East Asia, and to anyone who loves quiet, considered design. The Tokyo post in the gift ideas guide uses this theme, and the Paris Pinterest card in the layouts guide uses it as well.

One thing to watch: the red follows the major roads, so the accent shows up wherever the city has strong main routes. In a place with a clear arterial structure, such as Tokyo or Paris, it is magnificent. In a small town with few main roads, there will be less red to see.

20. Retro Vintage

Retro Vintage is described as antique parchment cartography with jade-seafoam water, olive parks and golden amber roads. The land is a parchment tan (#EBE3CD), the major roads are a golden amber (#F8C967), the secondary roads are a warm orange (#E98D58), the water is a seafoam green (#B9D3C2) and the parks are a clear olive (#A5B076). Text is a deep brown (#523735).

It looks like the map in an old travel book. It has the most colours of the heritage themes, and it is the one that treats green space as a feature. That makes it excellent for cities with big parks, such as London, New York or Paris with its gardens and the Bois, and for old towns that carry a sense of history.

It is a nostalgic choice and it is a great gift for a history lover, a collector of old maps or a person whose home is full of books.

21. Aurora

Aurora is the last theme in the list, and it is a dark theme with a cool, luminous character. In the Paris capture, the background is a very deep teal-black, and the roads glow in a bright mint-aqua, with the major roads thicker and brighter and the small streets fading into the dark. The result looks a little like the northern lights seen over a city.

It suits people who want a dark, glowing look but a calmer palette. Mint on teal is cooler and more serene than magenta on navy, and it feels modern without being aggressive. It is a natural fit for tech workspaces, desks and ultrawide monitors, and for northern places, where the colours echo the sky. The ultrawide desktop card in the layouts guide uses Aurora.

On a wall, it is dramatic and moody. On a screen, it glows.

Choosing a theme: five ways to decide

Twenty-one themes is a lot of choice. Rather than flipping through them at random, use one of these five routes to narrow the list. They overlap, so you can combine them.

Route one: start from the place

Think about what the place looks like and feels like. Some places already suggest a palette.

  • Coastal and harbour cities such as Lisbon, Sydney, Mumbai, Venice or Hong Kong invite Ocean or Blue Water, where the water becomes a feature. Midnight Blue also works well, because a dark sea beside gold roads is dramatic.
  • Old, historic cities such as Rome, Prague, Jaipur or Edinburgh suit Retro Vintage, Sandstone or Terracotta, whose warmth carries a sense of age.
  • Modern, glassy, high-energy cities such as Tokyo at night, Dubai, Singapore or Seoul suit Neon, Aurora, Noir or Midnight Blue.
  • Hill towns and green places such as Dehradun, Shimla, Ooty or a mountain village suit Sage, Watercolor Wash or Sandstone, where the calm palette matches the calm of the place.
  • Cities with a clear structure, such as Paris with its ring, Delhi with its central circle or New York with its grid, suit high-contrast themes like Noir, Pencil Sketch, Japanese Ink or Midnight Blue, which make the structure sing.
  • Small towns and villages have fewer roads, so they do well with colourful themes where each line carries more weight, such as Crayon Art, Pop Art or Chalkboard Cafe, or with calm themes where whitespace is part of the design.

Route two: start from the person

Think about who the poster is for, and what they are like. A person who loves quiet, neutral rooms will love Carrara, Sandstone or Japanese Ink. A person who has a bright, collected home, full of colour, will enjoy Terracotta, Retro Vintage or Pop Art. A person who loves technology and screens will enjoy Neon, Aurora or Blueprint. A person who is romantic and sentimental will appreciate Ruby, Sunset or Blush.

The best gifts reflect the receiver's taste, not the giver's. If you are not sure, look at what they already own: the colours of their sofa, their phone case, their favourite jumper. A person who always wears navy and gold will love Midnight Blue. A person with a houseplant in every corner will love Sage.

Route three: start from the room

If the poster is going on a wall, look at the wall.

  • Bright rooms with lots of white can carry almost anything, but light themes such as Carrara, Terracotta and Sunset look airy, while dark themes make a strong contrast.
  • Dark or moody rooms, such as a study with dark walls, welcome dark themes such as Noir, Midnight Blue and Ruby, and a light theme can look washed out.
  • Wood and natural materials go well with Terracotta, Sandstone, Sage and Retro Vintage.
  • Modern, monochrome interiors go well with Noir, Carrara, Pencil Sketch and Japanese Ink.
  • Children's rooms go well with Crayon Art, Pop Art and Watercolor Wash.

If you plan to frame the poster, think about the frame as part of the design. A black frame sharpens a light poster and blends into a dark one. A natural wood frame warms a cool poster. A white frame with a wide mount makes any theme look more like gallery art.

Route four: start from the screen

For a phone or desktop wallpaper, the rules are a little different, because screens add their own glow and their own clutter.

  • Dark themes look best on screens because they glow, save eye strain in low light, and make coloured lines pop. Noir, Midnight Blue, Neon, Aurora and Ruby are all strong choices.
  • Low-contrast themes, such as Sage, Blush and Sandstone, work as lock-screen wallpapers because they leave room for the clock and notifications.
  • High-contrast themes work for lock screens with minimal clutter and for desktops, but can make icons hard to read on a home screen.
  • For e-ink and greyscale screens, choose themes whose appeal depends on light and dark, not hue: Noir, Carrara, Pencil Sketch and Japanese Ink are the safest.

Route five: start from the occasion

Sometimes the occasion decides. A wedding anniversary might call for Midnight Blue or Ruby. A new home might call for Blueprint or Terracotta. A first date might call for Japanese Ink or Sunset. A baby's arrival might call for Watercolor Wash or Blush. A marathon or a mountain climb might call for Noir, Midnight Blue or Aurora, where a dark ground makes a line stand out. We go further into occasions in the gift ideas guide on this blog.

How to compare themes properly

When you have narrowed the list to two or three, compare them rigorously instead of by gut feel. Here is a short routine that takes a couple of minutes.

  1. Fix the place and the layout first. Compare themes on the same view of the same city, so the only thing changing is colour.
  2. Look at it small. Zoom out or step back from the screen. A good theme still reads as a clear, attractive pattern when it is tiny. A poor choice turns to mush or glare.
  3. Look at it large. Use the 4K preview to zoom in on the details. Check the text, the fine lines and the water.
  4. Check the text. Read the city name, country and coordinates. Make sure the text colour has good contrast against the background in the theme you chose.
  5. Look at the edges. See how the map fades or crops at the boundary. Some themes handle edges more gracefully than others.
  6. Come back later. Look again after an hour or the next day. First impressions of bright themes can be exciting and then fade, and quiet ones can feel dull at first and then grow on you.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

Choosing the theme before the place. A theme that looks wonderful on Paris may fall flat on a city with a very different street pattern. Always preview on your actual place.

Choosing the boldest theme for a long-term wall poster. Bright themes catch the eye, but they can wear thin. If the poster is for a home, consider whether you will enjoy it every day for years.

Ignoring the water. In a coastal city, the water covers a large part of the canvas. Check that the water colour works with the rest of the palette. In Sandstone, for example, the water is a tan that blends into the land, which can be lovely but also lose the coastline.

Forgetting the text. The name and coordinates are part of the design. Light text on a light background or dark on dark would be unreadable, but all of the built-in themes have been matched, so the more important question is whether you like the text colour.

Assuming light themes are safer. Light themes can look washed out on a bright screen, and low-contrast ones can disappear on a wall. Dark themes can look heavy. There is no universally safe option, only the one that suits your use.

Comparing at different zoom levels. If one theme is previewed zoomed in and another zoomed out, you are comparing the framing, not the colour. Keep the view the same.

Beyond the theme: what else changes the look

A theme gives you the base, but the editor has other tools that change the final result. You can search for a place or exact coordinates and adjust the view so the part of the city you care about is in the right place. You can style the borders and the text, so the poster has the framing and lettering you like. You can add markers to mark particular spots, such as the place you met, your first flat or the finish line. And if you are making something for a runner or a hiker, you can upload a GPX track so the actual route is drawn on the map.

Each of these adds a personal layer on top of the theme. A marker in a contrasting colour on a quiet theme can become a focal point. A route line on a dark theme glows. Use the theme to set the mood, and use the personal details to tell the story.

A note on printing and downloads

Maparte is a browser-based tool that gives you a digital file. We do not print or ship posters. If you plan to print, remember that colours on a screen can look different from colours on paper, and dark, saturated themes in particular can shift. A printer can usually advise on this. If you are making a wallpaper, none of this applies, because the colours you see on the screen are the colours you get.

Frequently asked questions

How many themes does Maparte have?

The theme picker in the editor includes twenty-one themes, from Midnight Blue to Aurora. They are all listed in this guide, with a Paris poster for each.

Can I preview a theme on my own city?

Yes. The 4K preview at maparte.com lets you search for any place, switch themes and see the result before you decide anything else.

Which theme is best for a wedding or anniversary gift?

There is no single answer, but good starting points are Midnight Blue for a classic, luxurious feel, Ruby for passion, Sunset or Blush for something softer, and Watercolor Wash for a delicate, romantic look. Choose the one that matches the couple's home and taste.

Which themes look best as a phone wallpaper?

Dark themes such as Noir, Midnight Blue and Aurora look striking on phone screens. Low-contrast themes such as Sage and Blush leave room for the clock and icons. Avoid very bright, high-contrast themes if you want the icons to stay readable.

Are Carrara and Pencil Sketch the same?

They are very close. Both use warm off-white backgrounds and charcoal lines. In our captures Pencil Sketch is slightly darker and crisper, and Carrara is a touch softer. Compare them on your own city to decide.

Which theme is best for a coastal city?

Ocean and Blue Water both make the water a feature. Midnight Blue and Blueprint are good if you prefer a dark look. Sandstone is less suitable, since its water is close in colour to its land.

Which themes work best for black-and-white or e-ink screens?

Noir, Carrara, Pencil Sketch and Japanese Ink rely on light and dark rather than colour, which makes them the safest choices for greyscale displays.

Does the theme change the map itself?

No. The streets, rivers and coastlines are the same in every theme. The theme only changes the colours and the style of the lines.

Where does the map data come from?

The map data is from OpenStreetMap, delivered through OpenFreeMap and drawn with MapLibre. Maparte credits these sources.
