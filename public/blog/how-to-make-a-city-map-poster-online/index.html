Making a city map poster used to mean learning mapping software or hiring a designer. Today you can do it in a browser in about ten minutes, as long as you know what choices you are making and why. This guide walks through the whole process in five steps, from typing a place name to downloading a file. It uses Maparte, the browser tool we make, so the screens are real. The ideas apply to any map poster maker, and where they do not, we say so.

A quick honesty note before we start. Maparte is a digital tool. You design in the browser, check a 4K preview and download a file. We do not print posters and we do not ship anything. If you want paper, you print the file yourself or take it to a print shop, and a later section explains how to do that well.

The five steps. 1. Pick the place and frame it. 2. Choose a layout. 3. Choose a theme and a border. 4. Edit the text and add markers or a route. 5. Check the 4K preview and download.

Before you start: five minutes of thinking

The best posters begin with a decision made before opening any tool. Spend five minutes on these questions and the rest becomes easy.

What is the poster for?

A gift, your own wall, a phone wallpaper, a social post and a race memento are different jobs. The job decides the shape of the image, the amount of text and even the colours. A wall poster can carry a title and a line of detail. A phone wallpaper needs quiet space where the clock and icons sit.

Which place, exactly?

"Paris" is a city, but the place you love may be a single street, a river crossing or a hill. Decide the exact spot. Write down the name as you want it to appear, including any accents. If it is a "where we met" poster, decide whether the focus is the city or the exact corner.

Where will it be displayed?

If you plan to frame it, measure the frame first and note the paper size. If it is a wallpaper, note the device model. This tells you which layout to choose in step 2.

What mood do you want?

Dark and dramatic, soft and warm, technical and blue, playful and bright. You do not need to name a theme yet, only a feeling. In step 3 you will match the feeling to a palette.

What text, if any?

A title, a subtitle and a short detail line, such as coordinates or a date. Write them down so you can paste them in without typing under pressure.

Step 1: pick the place and frame it

Open maparte.com. The landing page has a search field. Type the place and wait for suggestions.

Search is powered by a public place-search service, so it understands cities, towns, districts and many landmarks. If several places share a name, look at the extra text next to each suggestion, such as the region or the country, and choose the right one. If you pick the wrong one, search again. Nothing is lost.

Once the map moves to the place, spend a minute on the framing. Drag the map and zoom with the controls or your scroll wheel. Here are the framing rules that make the biggest difference.

  • Decide on one subject. A river, a harbour, a grid of streets, a park. Put the subject at the centre.
  • Zoom in more than feels natural. Wide views of whole regions become a soup of thin lines on paper. A tighter view shows the shape of streets, and the shape of streets is what makes a map poster beautiful.
  • Look at water and green space. Rivers, coasts and parks give the poster colour variety and a clear structure. A city on a river nearly always looks stronger than the same city framed so that the river is out of view.
  • Avoid the edge of the data. If you zoom out so far that the map shows empty land on one side, the composition will feel unbalanced.

The Paris example above shows what a good framing does. The river bends through the middle, the street pattern radiates around it, and nothing is cut awkwardly at the edge.

A tip for small towns

A small town has fewer roads. That is a strength, not a weakness. A clean layout with a lot of white space shows each road clearly. Zoom in until the town's main streets and any water fill most of the frame, then choose a calm theme. If your town is very small, try a portrait layout so the roads have room to breathe.

Step 2: choose a layout

A layout is the shape and size of your finished image. Maparte has 42 layouts in five groups: 18 print sizes, 10 social media shapes, 7 wallpaper formats, 4 iPhone wallpapers and 3 web formats. Open the layout panel and look at the list.

How to choose:

  1. Printing at home or at a shop? Pick the paper size you will use: A4 or Letter for home printers, A3 or larger for a shop. Use the same ratio as your frame.
  2. A phone wallpaper? Pick the layout for your device. There are separate iPhone layouts, a large Android phone layout and two iPad sizes.
  3. A desktop wallpaper? Choose Full HD, 4K or ultrawide, whichever matches your screen.
  4. A social post? Choose Instagram Square or Portrait, Story, a Pinterest Pin, or a LinkedIn or Twitter header, depending on where it will be posted.

Our layouts guide describes every layout and which kind of place suits it. For this walkthrough, a simple rule is enough: tall layouts suit tall subjects such as rivers and avenues, wide layouts suit coastlines and skylines, and a square suits compact places.

Changing the layout after you have framed the map will change how much of the map is visible, so check your framing again after you switch. It is quicker to pick the layout first, then frame the place.

Step 3: choose a theme and a border

The theme is the palette. Maparte has 22 themes, from dark and dramatic ones such as Midnight Blue, Noir and Neon to gentle ones such as Blush, Sage and Sandstone, and artistic ones such as Pencil Sketch, Crayon Art, Watercolor Wash, Japanese Ink and Retro Vintage.

A reliable method for choosing:

  1. Pick three candidates that match your mood. Do not try all 21 at once.
  2. Switch between them on the same framing. The change is instant, so comparison is easy.
  3. Look at the roads and the water. A good theme makes the main roads readable and keeps the water distinct from the land.
  4. Imagine the poster in its place. A dark theme on a pale wall looks bold. A pale theme in a dark room looks soft.
  5. Choose the one you keep returning to.

Borders

The border panel lists a row of frame styles: Single Line, Double Line, Thick Thin, Thin Thick, Modern Dashed, Fine Dotted, Dash Dot, Corner Crosshairs, Wide Gallery, Triple Line, Thin-Thick Thin and Line Dash-Dot, plus a clean full-bleed option with no border.

A few simple rules help:

  • A single or double line is classic. It suits almost every theme.
  • Wide Gallery leaves a wide margin and suits art-print style posters.
  • Corner Crosshairs has a technical look that goes well with Blueprint and Noir.
  • Full bleed is the best choice for wallpapers, because a border on a phone screen wastes space.
  • When in doubt, choose the simpler border. A busy border competes with the map.

Step 4: edit the text, add markers or a route

Text turns a map into a poster. Maparte lets you edit a title, a subtitle and detail lines, and choose from a list of typefaces. Everything updates on the map as you type.

Write the title

Keep it short. A city name in capitals is the classic choice. For a personal poster, a short phrase works well: the name of the place plus a date, or the names of two people. If the title is long, the typeface shrinks or wraps, so check the preview.

Add a subtitle and details

A subtitle can be the country, the neighbourhood or a short phrase. Detail lines often hold coordinates, a date or a distance. For a "where we met" poster, the date and the coordinates of the exact spot are a pleasing detail. The coordinates are shown in the standard latitude and longitude format.

Choose a typeface

There is a list to choose from, grouped as serif, sans serif, display and monospace. Serif typefaces feel classic and warm. Sans-serif typefaces feel modern and clean. A good rule is to use one typeface for the title and the same family for the details. Too many styles make a poster look cluttered.

Add markers

A marker points to a particular spot: the café, the station, the house. Add one when the poster is about a place within the city and not the city as a whole. Keep markers few. One or two make a point, and ten make a map of clutter.

Upload a GPX route

If you have a recording of a run, a ride or a hike, you can upload the GPX file and the route is drawn on the map. Most running and cycling watches and apps can export GPX. Frame the map so the whole route is visible, choose a theme with strong contrast between the roads and the line, and add the date and the distance as detail text. A separate marathon guide is planned for this.

Proofread

Read every word aloud. Check spelling and accents. Read the numbers twice. A typo on a poster is easy to fix before download and hard to fix after printing.

Step 5: check the 4K preview and download

When the design looks right, look at the 4K preview. This is the step that most people rush, and it is where the best results come from.

What to check

  1. Text. Is every word spelled correctly? Is the title the right size?
  2. Roads. Are the main streets visible and the small ones soft?
  3. Water and green. Do they contrast clearly with the land?
  4. Edges. Is anything cut off awkwardly at the border?
  5. Balance. Is the subject in the visual centre, and is the white space even?
  6. Room preview. If you want to see how it might look on a wall, try the optional room preview. It is a guide, and the real poster will vary with paper and light.

Download

Open the download panel. It lists the file formats on offer for your design.

Choose the format you need:

  • PNG is a safe general choice for digital use and for most print shops.
  • JPG is smaller and good for sharing on social media or by message.
  • TIFF is an uncompressed format that some print shops prefer.
  • A PNG and JPG pack gives you both images in one package.
  • PDF is a document file that is easy to send to a print shop or to open on any computer.
  • SVG is a vector file. It is not tied to one pixel size, so it can be scaled up for very large uses.
  • The all-in-one master bundle is a single ZIP with PNG, JPG, PDF and SVG together. TIFF is offered separately.

The panel also states the pixel size for your layout. For example, the A4 Portrait example shows 2480 by 3507 pixels, which matches A4 paper at a print-ready density. Download the file, and then save it in two places so it is not lost.

This guide does not state a price. Check the live site for the current terms on the download before you start, and be aware that the terms of use allow personal, non-commercial use, including printing your own copy, but not reselling files.

After the download: printing it well

If you want paper, you have two routes.

At home

A4 and Letter are easy. Use good paper (matte photo paper or a heavier art paper), set the printer to the highest quality, and print a test first. Use "actual size" or "100 percent", not "fit to page", unless your paper matches the layout.

At a print shop

Ask which file type they prefer, tell them the layout you chose, and ask for a small test or a paper sample. A good shop will advise on paper. Our guide to choosing a custom map poster maker lists the questions worth asking a print shop.

Framing

Measure the frame opening, not the outer edge. If the frame has a mat, the visible area is smaller than the paper. A simple wooden or black frame suits most themes. For wide layouts such as the 24 x 36 inch poster, a poster-hanger can be a simple option instead of a frame.

Common problems and quick fixes

  • The map looks too busy. Zoom in and choose a calmer theme, or use a larger layout.
  • The map looks empty. Zoom out a little or choose a layout with a different ratio.
  • The text is too small. Shorten it, or use a bigger layout.
  • The colours look different on paper. Screens glow and paper reflects. Print a small test and adjust the theme if needed.
  • The wrong place is shown. Search again and check the region next to each suggestion.
  • The title is cut off. Shorten the title or reduce the text size.
  • The wallpaper is covered by icons. Frame the map so the quiet area sits behind the clock and widgets.

What the map is actually drawing

A map poster is a picture of data, and knowing what the data holds helps you make better choices. Maparte draws its maps from OpenStreetMap data, delivered through OpenFreeMap tiles. OpenStreetMap is a community-built map of the world: volunteers add and correct roads, rivers, parks, buildings and place names. The poster credits OpenStreetMap contributors, as the data licence asks.

The layers you see

Most map posters show the same handful of layers, and each theme gives them different colours.

  • Land. The background, which sets the overall mood.
  • Water. Seas, lakes and rivers. Water is often the strongest shape on a map.
  • Parks and green space. Patches of green that break up a dense city.
  • Roads. Main roads are drawn heavier than small streets, which creates the hierarchy that makes a map readable.
  • Buildings and labels. In a close view, you may see building outlines and labels, depending on the theme and the zoom level.

Why two places can look so different

A planned grid city, a medieval centre and a coastal village each produce a different pattern. Grid cities give you calm, regular lines. Old centres give tangled, organic lines that many people find beautiful. Coastal places give a clean edge between land and sea. None of these is better. Choose the one that matches the story of your place.

Why some places look sparse

The map is only as detailed as the data. Many places are mapped very thoroughly, and some rural areas have fewer roads and labels. A sparse area can still make a lovely poster, because the few lines that exist have room to breathe. If you want more detail, zoom in on a denser part, such as a village centre.

Names and languages

Place names come from the data, and in some places they appear in the local language. If you want a different spelling for the title, type it yourself in the text fields. The title is yours to write.

Design principles that make a poster feel finished

You do not need training, but a few ideas from graphic design apply directly to map posters.

Hierarchy

Hierarchy means the eye knows where to look first. On a map poster, the title is usually first, the map second and the detail line third. If the detail text is as large as the title, the hierarchy is lost. Keep the title clearly the biggest text.

Contrast

Contrast is the difference between the lightest and darkest parts. Strong contrast makes roads easy to read. Very low contrast, such as a pale grey road on a pale cream land, looks elegant but can be hard to read at a distance. Look at the preview from a few steps back to test it.

Margins and breathing room

Give the map and the text space. A border and a margin make a poster look designed. If you use a full-bleed layout, such as for a wallpaper, make sure the important content is not too close to the edge, because phone screens have rounded corners.

Consistency

Use one typeface family, one border style and a restrained palette. The theme does most of this for you. The less you add, the more finished the result looks.

Restraint

The most common mistake is adding too much: three markers, a long dedication, a busy border and an unusual typeface. Remove one thing and look again. Often the poster improves.

Four worked examples, step by step

Here are four complete walkthroughs. The people and posters are examples, not real customers.

Example 1: a "where we met" poster

  1. Search the city, then zoom in on the street or the park where it happened.
  2. Choose a portrait layout, such as A4 Portrait.
  3. Choose a gentle theme, such as Blush or Sandstone.
  4. Add a marker on the exact spot.
  5. Title: the place name. Subtitle: the two names. Detail line: the date and the coordinates.
  6. Pick a single-line border, check the preview, and download.
  7. Print on matte paper, frame it, and hand it over with a note.

Example 2: a poster of your home town

  1. Search the town and frame its main streets and any river or coast.
  2. Choose Poster 18 x 24 in, or a size that matches your frame.
  3. Choose a theme that suits the room: Midnight Blue for a bold wall, Sage for a calm one.
  4. Title: the town name. Subtitle: the region or the year you moved.
  5. Download, and ask a print shop to print it on heavier paper.

Example 3: a travel memory

  1. Search the main city of your trip. If you travelled between cities, choose the one that was the heart of the journey.
  2. Choose a landscape or square layout.
  3. Choose Watercolor Wash or Retro Vintage for a soft, nostalgic mood.
  4. Title: the city. Subtitle: the month and year. Add a marker for your favourite place.
  5. Download and print an A4 copy for a desk or a shelf.

Example 4: a phone wallpaper of a favourite city

  1. Search the city and frame the area you know best.
  2. Choose the layout for your device, for example an iPhone layout.
  3. Choose a dark, calm theme so icons and the clock remain legible.
  4. Use full bleed, with no border.
  5. Keep the text minimal, or none at all. Leave a quiet area at the top for the clock.
  6. Download the PNG and set it as your wallpaper.

Making the same poster for several uses

One of the benefits of a digital tool is that one design can become several products. A typical set might be:

  • An A4 Portrait for a frame on a shelf.
  • An Instagram Square to share on a feed.
  • An iPhone layout as a wallpaper.
  • A Pinterest Pin shape to save and share.

Each layout reframes the map, so check the preview for each one. The theme and the text can stay the same, which keeps the set looking consistent.

A thirty-minute plan for a gift

If you are making a gift against the clock, here is a realistic plan.

  1. Minutes 0 to 5. Decide the place, the recipient's taste and the layout.
  2. Minutes 5 to 15. Search, frame and try three themes.
  3. Minutes 15 to 22. Write the text, add a marker, and proofread twice.
  4. Minutes 22 to 27. Check the 4K preview, and download.
  5. Minutes 27 to 30. Send the file as a digital gift, or send it to a print shop.

A digital gift can be received the same day. A printed one depends on how fast your print shop works.

Choosing the right place when you have too many

Many people stall at step one, not because search is hard, but because the place is. If a city, a street and a neighbourhood all matter, here is how to decide.

Ask what the poster should say

A map of a whole city says "this is where I am from" or "this is where we travelled". A map of a street says "this is where I live". A map of one corner says "this is the exact spot". Pick the statement you want to make, and the scale follows.

Imagine the title

If the title you picture is a city name, frame the city. If it is a street name, frame the street. If it is two names and a date, frame the exact spot and add a marker. The title and the framing should agree.

Consider the shape

Some places have a strong shape: an island, a peninsula, a river loop. These give a poster a clear silhouette. A place without an obvious shape can still work, if you choose a theme with good contrast and a calm border.

If there are two places

Two places that matter, such as where each of you grew up, can be two posters in a matching pair. Use the same layout, theme and typeface for both, and change only the place. Hung side by side, they read as a set.

If you cannot decide

Make two designs. Looking at both previews makes the choice clear in a minute, and you can explore in the preview as long as you like.

A simple vocabulary for talking to a print shop or a framer

When you take a file to a shop, a few words make the conversation easier.

  • Bleed: extra image that extends past the trim edge, so that cutting does not leave a white edge. For a poster with a border inside the picture, you usually do not need it.
  • Matte, satin, gloss: the surface finish of the paper. Matte reflects little light, gloss reflects the most.
  • Weight (gsm): how heavy the paper is. A heavier paper feels more substantial.
  • Mat or mount: a card frame inside the frame that surrounds the picture. It reduces the visible area.
  • Aspect ratio: the proportion of width to height. A4 and A3 share a ratio, so you can scale one to the other without cropping.

If the shop uses a term you do not know, ask. A good shop is happy to explain.

Reusing a design later

A design you make today may be useful again. In a year you may want an anniversary update, a second copy for a relative, or a version in another size. If you keep notes on the place, the layout, the theme and the text, you can recreate it in minutes. If you plan a set, such as one poster per year or one per city you have lived in, using the same layout and theme each time gives the set a pleasing unity.

Colour on screen and colour on paper

A screen emits light and paper reflects it, so the same file looks different in the two places. Knowing the differences prevents surprises.

  • Dark themes look deeper on screen. A glowing near-black background looks inky on a phone and can look softer on paper. Matte paper keeps dark areas from reflecting room light.
  • Bright colours soften on paper. A neon green or electric blue is vivid on screen and calmer in print. If you want impact on paper, choose a theme with strong value contrast, meaning a clear difference between light and dark, not only a bright hue.
  • Pale lines can disappear. A thin pale road on a cream land can fade in print. Check the preview at full size, and consider a theme with a darker road colour for large prints.
  • Lighting changes everything. A poster in warm evening light looks different from the same poster in cool daylight. If you can, view the print where it will hang.

A small test print on the paper you plan to use is the best way to learn how a theme behaves. Most shops will print an A4 test for a small fee, or you can print one at home.

Reading the preview like a proofreader

Proofreading is a skill, and a poster rewards it. Try this routine.

  1. Read the title alone. Is each letter correct? Are accents in the right place?
  2. Read the subtitle and detail lines. Are the date and the numbers correct? Read each number backwards, digit by digit.
  3. Check the coordinates. If you add them, check which is latitude and which is longitude, and the sign for hemisphere.
  4. Look at the edges. Is any label cut off by the border?
  5. Look at the map itself. Is the subject where you intended? Is any important street missing from the view?
  6. Ask a second person. A fresh pair of eyes finds mistakes you can no longer see.

Fixing a mistake in the editor takes seconds. Fixing it after a print costs paper and time.

Typefaces in more depth

The editor offers a list of typefaces, grouped as serif, sans serif, display and monospace. The choice affects the mood more than people expect.

  • Serif faces have small strokes at the ends of letters. They feel traditional, literary and warm, and they suit wedding and anniversary posters.
  • Sans-serif faces are clean and modern. They suit wallpapers, city posters with a contemporary look and technical themes such as Blueprint.
  • Display faces have strong personality. Use them for a short title only, and pair them with a quiet face for details.

Test the title in two or three faces and view each from a distance. Choose the one that stays legible. Avoid using a very light weight on a dark theme, because thin strokes can disappear.

Wallpaper-specific tips

A wallpaper has different needs from a poster.

  • Use full bleed. A border on a phone screen wastes precious space.
  • Leave a quiet area. The top of a phone shows the clock and the bottom shows a dock. Frame the map so that the busiest part sits in the middle.
  • Choose calm contrast. A wallpaper sits behind icons, so very bright lines can fight with them. Dark or muted themes tend to make icons easier to read.
  • Skip long text. A wallpaper rarely needs more than a short title, or none.
  • Match the device. Choose the layout for your exact device, so that nothing is cropped.

Our separate wallpaper guide covers the sizes in detail.

File care: naming, saving and backing up

A poster you love is worth keeping safe. Use a name that tells you what it is, such as the place, the layout and the theme, for example "paris-a4-portrait-terracotta.png". Save the file in two places, such as your device and a cloud folder, and keep the final version separate from drafts. If you plan to make changes later, note the title, the theme and the layout somewhere, so you can recreate the design quickly. If you use a print shop, keep the file they printed, so a reprint matches.

Mistakes people make, and how to avoid them

  1. Choosing a theme before the framing. Frame first. A good framing improves every theme.
  2. Adding too much text. One title and one detail line is often enough.
  3. Picking the wrong layout for the frame. Match the ratio and the size.
  4. Forgetting to proofread. Read the poster twice, then ask a friend.
  5. Using a wide view of a big region. Zoom in on the shape that matters.
  6. Printing without a test. One small test saves a large mistake.
  7. Ignoring the terms. Personal use is fine. Reselling files is not.
  8. Saving the file in only one place. Keep a backup.

A ten-minute checklist before you download

Before you press download, run through this list once. It catches nearly every problem people hit with a first poster, and each check takes under a minute.

  1. Read the title aloud. Spelling mistakes in a place name are easy to miss on screen, and hard to forgive on paper.
  2. Check the coordinates or date line. If you added one, confirm the digits. A swapped number looks like a real location, so it is the kind of error nobody notices until it is framed.
  3. Zoom to the corners. Look at the four corners of the preview. Labels sometimes sit close to the edge, and a frame or a mat will hide a little of the border.
  4. Look at the data credit. It should be present and readable but small.
  5. Compare two themes once more. After ten minutes of editing, your eye gets tired. Switch to your second choice and back, and notice which you prefer with fresh eyes.
  6. Confirm the layout matches the frame. An A4 Portrait file belongs in an A4 frame. A different frame means a different layout, so choose it first rather than last.
  7. Download the format that suits the use. PNG for a sharp file, JPG for a smaller one to share.
  8. Save the file with a clear name. Include the place, the layout and the theme, so you can find it in a year.
  9. Keep the project settings in your notes. If you want to make a matching poster later, you will want to remember the theme and the typeface.

If you are making the poster as a gift, add one more step. Look at it once as the person who will receive it, and ask whether the place, the words and the colours say what you meant.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take?

Most people can make a first design in about ten minutes. The time goes into framing and proofreading, not clicking.

Do I need an account?

You can look around and design without one. Check the live site for what the download needs.

Is the preview really 4K?

The editor shows a 4K preview. The download panel lists the pixel size for your chosen layout, so you can check it before you download.

Can I change the colours of a theme?

Maparte has 22 themes to choose from. Check the editor for what can be adjusted.

Can I search for a small village?

Yes, as long as it appears in the place-search results. If it does not, search for the nearest town and move the map by hand.

Which layout should I pick for a gift?

Pick the size of the frame you plan to use. A4 Portrait is a good default.

Can I use my GPX route?

Yes, upload the file and the route is drawn on the map.

Does Maparte print and ship?

No. It is a digital tool, and you print the file yourself or at a print shop.

Where does the map data come from?

OpenStreetMap contributors, delivered by OpenFreeMap tiles.

Can I sell the posters I make?

The terms allow personal, non-commercial use, and do not allow reselling files. Contact support for anything else.

A calm way to finish

You do not need design training to make a good map poster. You need one place that matters to you, a framing with a clear subject, a theme that matches the mood, and a proofread title. Open maparte.com, follow the five steps, and look at the 4K preview. If you like what you see, download it. If you do not, change one thing and look again.
