Map Poster Layouts and Sizes: A Complete Guide

Every layout in the Maparte editor, with the real names and sizes, and how to match a shape to a phone, a desktop, a feed or a frame.

Most people start a map poster by choosing a city, and that is a perfectly good place to begin. But the decision that quietly shapes the result more than any other is the one that comes next: the layout. A layout is the shape of the canvas, and the shape decides which part of your city survives inside it. A tall, narrow phone wallpaper and a wide desktop banner can be made from exactly the same coordinates and still look like two different places, because one shows a vertical slice of streets and the other shows a horizontal strip.

This guide walks through every layout family in the Maparte editor, using the real names and the real numbers from the layout picker. It explains what each shape is good for, where it tends to go wrong, and how to match a shape to the place where the finished design will actually live: a phone lock screen, a desk monitor, an Instagram feed, a Pinterest board, a frame on a wall, or a wallpaper for a tablet. Along the way you will see real posters made in the editor, so you can judge the shapes with your own eyes rather than taking our word for it.

One note before we start. Maparte is a digital tool: you design in the browser, preview in 4K, and download a digital file. We do not print or ship anything. Several of the layouts are named after paper sizes because those proportions are what frame shops, stationery and furniture stores are built around, and choosing the right proportion up front means a file will sit properly in a frame later, whoever does the printing. That is the only reason paper sizes appear in a digital editor, and we will say it again where it matters.

How the layout picker is organised

When you open the editor and look for the layout control, you will find the shapes grouped into families rather than thrown into one long list. At the time of writing the picker has five groups: Print, Social Media, Wallpaper, iPhone Wallpaper and Web. The grouping is useful because each family answers a different question.

  • Print answers "what proportion will my frame or paper have?" Its entries are named for paper and poster sizes and are measured in centimetres.
  • Social Media answers "where will this be posted?" Its entries are named for platforms and are measured in pixels.
  • Wallpaper answers "which screen will this sit on?" It covers desktop monitors, a large Android phone, two iPad sizes and an e-ink reader, all in pixels.
  • iPhone Wallpaper is a family of its own because each iPhone generation has a slightly different screen, and a wallpaper that is a few pixels off gets cropped or scaled.
  • Web answers "which slot on a website will this fill?" It holds a hero banner, a blog cover card and a dashboard card.

The two kinds of units matter. Print layouts are defined by physical size in centimetres, so they describe a proportion and a paper size at once. Everything else is defined in pixels, which means the number you see is the exact width and height of the file you are designing for. If you remember nothing else from this section, remember that distinction: centimetres describe a shape you will hold, pixels describe a screen you will look at.

The editor opens on A4 Portrait by default, which is a sensible starting point. It is a tall rectangle that works for a wall, a desk frame or a screen, and nearly everyone has a frame that fits something close to it.

Why shape changes the map more than you expect

A map poster is not a photo that gets cropped to fit. When you change layout in the editor, the canvas changes shape around your chosen centre point, and the amount of city that fits changes with it. Think of the map as a window onto a very large sheet of streets. A square window shows a square patch. A tall window shows a tall patch. A very wide window shows a thin horizontal band.

This has consequences that are easy to miss until you see them side by side. The posters below are all exports from the Maparte editor, each in a different layout. Notice how the same product produces such different compositions.

Look at the Paris A4 and the Mumbai square. Both show a whole city, but the tall frame gives Paris its ring road with room above and below, while the square gives Mumbai's peninsula equal room on the east and west, so the harbour and the creeks both appear. Now look at the ultrawide strip, where New York is reduced to a thin band and only part of the area is visible at all.

None of these is superior to the others. They are different answers to different questions, and the skill is in choosing the shape that suits what you want the viewer to see.

Three things that stay constant across shapes

Across all of these posters, a few design choices hold steady, and they are worth noticing because they are what makes a Maparte poster recognisable. The city name sits near the bottom in wide-spaced capital letters. Beneath it are the country and the exact coordinates in small type, divided from the name by a thin rule. And in the bottom corner, in small monospaced letters, is the maparte.com credit. These elements stay put as the canvas changes shape, so the layout you choose changes how much map you see while the typographic furniture stays consistent.

The practical consequence is that the lower part of a tall poster is partly taken up by the text block. If there is a particular street or building you care about, place it in the upper two thirds of the frame rather than right at the foot.

Print layouts: the paper-proportion family

The Print family is the largest, with eighteen entries, and it is the one most people think of when they hear the words "map poster". Remember that these layouts exist to give your design the right proportions; Maparte delivers a digital file and does not print or ship. What happens to the file afterwards is up to you.

The A series and why it is so convenient

The A-series is the international paper standard used across Europe, India, Canada and most of the world outside the United States. Its defining feature is a ratio of about 1 to 1.414, which is the square root of two. That ratio has a handy property: fold or cut an A4 sheet in half across its long side and you get two A5 sheets with exactly the same proportions. Because the shape never changes, a design that works at A4 works at A3, A2 and A1 with no recomposition at all. Only the physical size changes.

The editor includes A0 through A5 in portrait, plus A4 Landscape:

  • A0 Portrait, 84.1 × 118.9 cm. The largest in the set, about one square metre in area. It is a very large shape for an architectural or shop-window feel, and it is rarely what a home needs.
  • A1 Portrait, 59.4 × 84.1 cm. A big statement piece for a living room wall, roughly 23 × 33 inches.
  • A2 Portrait, 42 × 59.4 cm. A strong choice for a feature wall above a sofa or a bed.
  • A3 Portrait, 29.7 × 42 cm. Large enough to read as art and small enough for a hallway, a study or a bedroom.
  • A4 Portrait, 21 × 29.7 cm. The default. It suits desk frames, shelves and, because it is the same shape as a sheet of paper, it is also the easiest one to view, share and send around on a screen.
  • A5 Portrait, 14.8 × 21 cm. A small card-sized shape suited to a bedside table, a greeting-card style gift or a gallery-wall filler.
  • A4 Landscape, 29.7 × 21 cm. The same sheet turned on its side, for cities that are naturally wide, such as a long coastline or a river valley.

The A4 Portrait example above shows Paris in the Midnight Blue theme, 21 × 29.7 cm. That is a good example of why A4 is the default: the shape is tall enough to give a dense city room to breathe, and the caption block fits neatly at the foot without crowding the map.

US sizes: Letter, Legal and Tabloid

If you are in the United States or Canada, your household stationery and frame stock is built on a different family. The editor includes three of those sizes.

  • Letter (US) Portrait, 21.6 × 27.9 cm (8.5 × 11 in). Slightly wider and shorter than A4. It is the shape of everyday American paper and fits the standard frames sold at most home stores.
  • Legal (US) Portrait, 21.59 × 35.56 cm (8.5 × 14 in). The same width as Letter but noticeably longer. It is a tall, slender shape that suits a river or a long avenue.
  • Tabloid (US) Portrait, 27.94 × 43.18 cm (11 × 17 in). Twice the area of Letter, a comfortable step up for a wall.

The difference between A4 and Letter is small, about half a centimetre in width and nearly two centimetres in height (Letter is wider and shorter), but it matters if you are buying a frame. A4 artwork in a Letter frame will leave a thin margin at the sides, and Letter artwork in an A4 frame will be trimmed or shrunk. If you know where you live, pick the family that matches your frame shelf.

Poster sizes in inches and the 2:3 favourite

The next group uses the sizes that poster and frame manufacturers have standardised in inches.

  • Poster 12 × 18 in, 30.48 × 45.72 cm. A medium poster at a 2:3 ratio.
  • Poster 18 × 24 in, 45.72 × 60.96 cm. A large poster at a 3:4 ratio, a little squarer than 2:3.
  • Poster 24 × 36 in, 60.96 × 91.44 cm. The extra-large classic, 2:3 again. The editor describes it as a common extra-large poster print size.

The Poster 24 × 36 layout is 60.96 × 91.44 cm. At this ratio the shape is lean and tall, which is flattering to cities built along a spine, such as a peninsula or a river. It is also the same 2:3 ratio as the Pinterest Pin layout and the 12 × 18 poster, which gives you a nice option: design once at 2:3 and the composition travels across all three.

Metric gallery sizes: 50 × 70 and 70 × 100

The last pair are the metric gallery sizes, and they come with a useful hint in their descriptions. Poster 50 × 70 cm (B2) is described in the editor as a popular global gallery poster frame size, with retail frame ranges built around it. Poster 70 × 100 cm (B1) is described as a large statement poster frame size. These are the sizes you will often see in frames at Scandinavian and European home stores, and they are everywhere in India and the UK too. At 5:7 and 7:10, they are a little stockier than the 2:3 posters, which gives a city more width to spread into.

Caption layouts: three special A4 designs

Three of the print layouts do not simply change the proportions; they change the arrangement of the poster itself.

  • Square Caption · A4 (21 × 29.7 cm) places a square map above an editable, centred place caption. Think of a classic photo-print layout: a square picture with a generous label underneath.
  • Corner Details · A4 (21 × 29.7 cm) puts the place details in the upper-left corner, above a larger square map. It feels modern and editorial, like the title block on an architect's drawing.
  • Square Caption · A4 Landscape (29.7 × 21 cm) sets a square map on the left with the place details alongside, which suits a wide frame or a desk where a landscape piece reads more naturally.

These are the ones to pick when the words matter as much as the map: a couple's names and a date, a home address, a race result, a short phrase. In the standard layouts the text sits inside the map composition. In the caption layouts it has its own room, and the map is cropped to a calmer square.

Choosing within the Print family

A short way to choose: start from the frame you own or plan to use. If you do not have a frame yet, A4 Portrait for desks and shelves, A3 or 12 × 18 for a wall, and 24 × 36 or 70 × 100 cm if you want a big statement. If the design is going to stay on screens, keep A4 Portrait because it is the default and works as a shareable preview. And if you want text to share the stage, try one of the caption layouts.

A reminder on files. Maparte gives you a digital download. The paper sizes help you pick the right proportion for a frame or a mount, but we do not print or ship, so choose any print shop you like if you decide to print.

Desktop wallpaper layouts

The Wallpaper family starts with three monitor shapes. If you spend your day at a computer, this is where a city map earns its keep, because you see it every time you minimise a window.

Desktop Full HD, 1920 × 1080 px

This is the 16:9 shape of most laptops and standard monitors, and the safest choice if you are not sure what you have. The editor describes it as a 16:9 desktop wallpaper for standard monitors. It is a wide rectangle, so it favours cities with strong horizontal structure or a clear centre, where the eye can settle in the middle and the streets fan out sideways.

The Full HD example above shows Greater London in the Noir theme. It is a good demonstration of how a wide frame can work: the Thames bends through the middle of the picture, white and silver roads fan out on both sides, and the city name sits low and centred. A wide frame like this shows the spread of a city rather than a single neighbourhood. If your city has a strong river or coast, the wide shape gives that feature room to run across the screen.

A desktop wallpaper has a practical constraint that a poster does not: icons. Most operating systems lay icons out along the left edge and the bottom taskbar or dock covers the lower strip. A caption block at the bottom of the poster can end up hidden or fighting with icons. Moving the map's key subject a little to the right of centre and up from the bottom edge is a simple fix.

Desktop 4K, 3840 × 2160 px

This is the same 16:9 proportion as Full HD but with four times the pixels, described in the editor as a 4K UHD wallpaper. Choose it if you own a 4K monitor or a recent MacBook or high-resolution laptop and want line work to stay crisp when it is displayed at that sharpness. Because the ratio is identical to Full HD, a design composed at one translates directly to the other. The only reason to pick Full HD over 4K is if you want a lighter file.

Desktop Ultrawide, 3440 × 1440 px

This is the layout for the extra-wide monitors that gamers and designers favour, described in the editor as an ultrawide wallpaper for 21:9-style monitors. It is a hard shape for a map, and a rewarding one. The canvas is more than twice as wide as it is tall, so only a thin horizontal band of the city fits. Look at the New York example in the gallery above: the harbour, the rivers and the island share one long strip in soft green on pale sage, with the name small and centred. There is no room for the whole region, and that is the point. The ultrawide format turns the map into texture, a pattern that stretches across the screen the way a panoramic photograph does.

A few tips for ultrawide specifically. Choose a place with a long, interesting horizontal feature, such as a river, a coast, a causeway or a railway line, so the band has a story. Choose a darker theme, since large bright areas on a big monitor can be tiring to look at all day. And zoom out a little more than you think you need to, because the strip crops away the top and bottom so aggressively that a tight zoom can end up as abstract lines.

Phone and tablet wallpaper layouts

Your phone is the screen you look at most, which is why a map of a meaningful place is such a popular wallpaper. It is also where getting the exact size matters most, because phones have unusual proportions.

The iPhone Wallpaper family exists because Apple's screens are not all the same. Four layouts cover the current range, and the editor spells out which phones each one is for.

The reason to be exact is simple. If your wallpaper is made for a different screen than the one it ends up on, the phone has to scale or crop it, and the part it crops is often the part you cared about. A wallpaper made at the right size lines up with the lock screen as intended, so the clock and widgets land where you planned.

The Tokyo example above shows this family in the Terracotta theme on the iPhone 16/17 Pro layout (1206 × 2622 px). It is a quiet design: cream and clay lines, pale water, and the city name low on the canvas. That calm is deliberate. A lock screen is already busy with a clock, notifications and sometimes widgets, and a dense map competes with all of them.

To choose the right layout, open your phone's settings and look up the model name, then match it against the table. If your phone is not listed because it is older or a different make, choose the nearest tall layout. A wallpaper that is a close shape will usually be scaled to fit without much loss.

Designing for the lock screen versus the home screen

A phone wallpaper has two jobs, and they pull in different directions.

On the lock screen, the top third is dominated by the date and time, and the bottom corners usually carry shortcuts. The visual space in the middle is the most valuable, so put your subject, the neighbourhood or street you care about, in the centre band. Keep the poster's text block small and low, as in the Dehradun card, so it does not collide with the controls near the bottom.

On the home screen, icons cover most of the canvas in a grid. Detailed line work becomes noise behind app icons. For a home screen, a darker theme with low contrast between the map and the background reads as a subtle texture, while a high contrast theme can make icon labels hard to read.

If you use the same image for both, favour a low-contrast theme such as Sage, Midnight Blue or Noir, and keep the map simple. If you want to go big on a detailed, high-contrast design, make it for the lock screen only.

Galaxy S24 Ultra, 1440 × 3120 px

Android phones vary even more than iPhones, and the editor includes one Android layout: the Galaxy S24 Ultra at 1440 × 3120 px. It is taller and narrower than the iPhone shapes, at an aspect closer to 9:19.5. If you own a different Android phone, you can still use it as a starting point, because most modern phones sit in the same tall neighbourhood. Check the pixel dimensions of your screen in the phone's specifications and choose the nearest match.

iPad Pro 11" and iPad Pro 12.9"

Tablets are the odd ones in the wallpaper group, because they are used in both orientations. The editor offers the portrait shapes: 1668 × 2388 px for the 11-inch iPad Pro and 2048 × 2732 px for the 12.9-inch model. Both are close to 3:4, much squarer than a phone, and that has a happy side effect for maps. A 3:4 canvas shows a generous block of city in both directions, so the result looks like a proper map instead of a long strip.

Because a tablet is often set on a stand or a keyboard dock and used in landscape, think about which orientation you use most. If you mostly use it upright, the portrait layouts are a perfect fit. If you mostly use it sideways, the 16:9 Desktop Full HD or 4K layouts are a closer match, and the system will fit them to the screen.

Xteink X4 (E-Ink), 480 × 800 px

The most unusual layout in the list is the Xteink X4, a small e-ink screen at 480 × 800 px. It is described in the editor as the native format for that device's E-Ink display. E-ink screens show greys and a limited palette, and they refresh slowly, so a map designed for one benefits from bold, simple shapes and strong contrast. Pick a theme with a clear light and dark separation, such as Noir, Pencil Sketch or Japanese Ink, and avoid themes whose charm depends on subtle colour differences, since those will flatten into similar greys.

You may never use this layout, but it is a good reminder of what a layout really is: a statement about the display where the design will live. The more precisely you match the screen, the better the result.

Social media layouts

The Social Media family has ten layouts, measured in pixels. They are the right choice when the map is going to be posted rather than hung, whether as a feed post, a story, a pin, a cover or a thumbnail.

Instagram Square, 1080 × 1080 px

The classic 1:1 feed format. A square is one of the kindest shapes for a map, because the street pattern of most cities spreads in all directions and a square gives it equal room on each axis. Look back at the Paris Terracotta card: the whole inner city is visible, with the river curving through it, and nothing feels cut off. If you want to post a single city and have it read at a glance in a feed, this is the safest layout.

The Instagram feed currently also supports taller images, and that is where Instagram Portrait comes in.

Instagram Portrait, 1080 × 1350 px

At a 4:5 ratio, the portrait post takes up more vertical space in the feed than a square, which means more of the screen belongs to your image while someone scrolls. For a map, that extra height is an opportunity to include more of a vertical feature, such as a long coastline or a river, and still have room for the title block. If you are making a series of posts and want them to look like a consistent grid, choose one shape and stay with it.

Story (9:16), 1080 × 1920 px

The editor describes this one as a 9:16 vertical short-form format, which also covers vertical video platforms. It is the shape of a phone screen held upright, so it behaves like the phone wallpaper layouts but is a little shorter, with room in the upper and lower parts of the frame left free for platform interface elements.

When you design for a story, remember that the platform overlays its own interface. The top band is covered by the profile name and progress bar, and the bottom band by the reply field. Keep important words and the city name away from the very top and very bottom, and keep the map's focal point in the middle.

Pinterest Pin, 1000 × 1500 px

A 2:3 vertical format, the shape Pinterest favours. Tall pins take up more space in the grid than wide ones, so a map poster in this shape stands out on a board, particularly one in a clear colour that stands apart from neighbouring pins. The Delhi Japanese Ink example in the gallery earlier is light and airy, which is a good fit for a feed where many pins compete for attention. Because Pinterest users save images to return to later, a pin has a longer life than a feed post, so a clean, readable title block is worth the effort. The 2:3 ratio is also the shape of the 12 × 18 and 24 × 36 inch poster layouts, so the same composition works if you decide to use it as a poster too.

LinkedIn Post, LinkedIn Cover, Twitter Header and Reddit Banner

These four are the long, thin shapes of profile headers and wide feed images.

  • LinkedIn Post, 1200 × 627 px, is a landscape card, a little over 1.9:1.
  • LinkedIn Cover, 1584 × 396 px, is a 4:1 strip across the top of a profile or company page.
  • Twitter Header, 1500 × 500 px, is a 3:1 banner.
  • Reddit Banner, 1920 × 256 px, is a very thin 7.5:1 band at the top of a subreddit.

On strips like these, a map becomes a graphic texture. The city name and details would be tiny, so the best approach is to treat the map as a pattern and use the title only if it stays legible. Also remember that profile pictures and platform buttons overlap the strip, usually at the left or the bottom. Keep the interesting part of the map toward the centre and right.

A map of your home town as a header strip is a quiet personal touch for a freelancer, a small shop or a community group, and it takes only a minute to set up.

YouTube Thumbnail and Reddit Post (16:9)

The YouTube Thumbnail, 1280 × 720 px, and Reddit Post (16:9), 1200 × 675 px, share the same widescreen proportion as the desktop wallpapers. They work best with a map that has a strong, simple focal point, because thumbnails are viewed very small. For a thumbnail, choose a high-contrast theme and place the subject large; fine street detail disappears at thumbnail size.

Web layouts

The Web family is the smallest, with three layouts, but they are useful if you run a site, a blog or a small business.

  • Hero Banner, 1440 × 600 px, is the wide section at the top of a landing page. Like the ultrawide wallpaper, it turns the map into a band, and works best with space for text on one side.
  • Blog Featured, 1200 × 630 px, is described as a typical blog cover and Open Graph card, which means the image that appears when a link is shared on social platforms or in a chat. If you want a link to your own site to look good when shared, this is the shape to use.
  • Dashboard Card, 1280 × 960 px, is a balanced 4:3 frame for an app or dashboard visual.

For a blogger or a small business, a map of the shop's neighbourhood as a featured image stands in well for a stock photo, and it is a visual that nobody else has.

Choosing the right layout: a practical decision guide

With so many options, it helps to ask three questions in order.

  1. Where will it be seen? A screen, a feed, a frame or a website? That alone narrows the list to one family.
  2. What is the exact device or slot? Within the family, match the exact phone, monitor, platform or frame.
  3. What is the subject? A round city, a long river, a coastline, a single street or a district. Match the subject's shape to the canvas.

Matching the layout to the shape of the city

Cities are not rectangles, and some cities fit some canvases more naturally than others.

  • Round or radial cities with a strong centre, such as Paris with its ring or Delhi with its central hub, are at home in squares and in wide 16:9 frames, where the pattern spreads evenly.
  • Peninsulas and long narrow cities, such as Mumbai, are flattered by tall canvases like 24 × 36 in or phone wallpapers, which follow the length of the land.
  • Coastal and riverside places work in ultrawide and banner shapes, where the shoreline becomes the story.
  • Grid cities produce a calm, regular texture at almost any shape, which makes them a good choice for wallpaper.
  • Mountain towns and small places often have few roads. They look best on a tall, airy canvas where whitespace is part of the design, as in the Dehradun card.

The same city in several layouts

One of the best habits you can build is to make more than one layout of the same place. The editor keeps your settings, so changing layout is a matter of a couple of taps, and the preview updates at once. Try this: begin with the A4 Portrait for a gift or a framed version, then switch to the iPhone layout for a lock screen, then Instagram Square to share it. You end up with a set of matching pieces from a single design session, and the whole family looks consistent because the theme and the place are the same.

Practical tips for every layout

Preview before you commit. Maparte shows a 4K preview, so you can look at the full design on screen at a high level of detail before you do anything else. Zoom in on small details, such as text and thin lines, and check that nothing is cut off at the edges.

Check the margins. Posters need a safe margin, because frames and mounts cover the outer edge, and screens crop their corners. Keep important text and details away from the edges.

Think about the text. A place name that fits comfortably on a tall layout may feel cramped on a square or a strip. If the name is long, try the caption layouts, which have their own text area.

Keep contrast in mind. On a small screen, high-contrast themes are easier to read. On a big wall, subtle themes can look elegant, but they need good lighting.

Use the map controls. You can search for a place or coordinates, adjust the view, add markers for particular spots and style borders and text, so the same city can be framed differently for each layout.

Remember it is digital. Whatever layout you choose, you get a digital file. Choose a layout for the way you will use it, and if you want it printed, the paper-sized layouts will make sure the proportions are ready for it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the default layout in the Maparte editor?

The default is A4 Portrait, 21 × 29.7 cm. It is a good all-round shape for a desk, a shelf or a screen.

Which layout should I choose for my phone?

Choose the iPhone or Galaxy layout that matches your phone's screen. For recent iPhones, the 16/17 Pro and iPhone 17 layout is 1206 × 2622 px. If you are not sure which size matches, check the model name in your phone's settings and compare it with the layout names in the picker.

Can I see the whole design before I download?

Yes. The editor shows a 4K preview at maparte.com, so you can design and look at your poster in detail before deciding anything further. Check the live site for the current terms on downloads.

Does Maparte print and ship posters?

No. Maparte is a digital tool and provides digital downloads only. The paper-size layouts, such as A4 and 24 × 36 inch, exist so that the proportions suit frames, and any printing is something you arrange yourself.

Can I make the same design in several layouts?

Yes. Change the layout in the editor and the design adapts to the new shape. You may need to adjust the map view slightly, because a different shape shows a different amount of the city.

What is the difference between A4 and US Letter?

A4 is 21 × 29.7 cm and Letter is 21.6 × 27.9 cm. Letter is a little wider and noticeably shorter. Choose whichever matches the frames in your country.

Which layout is best for a big wall?

Look at the 24 × 36 inch, 70 × 100 cm (B1) and A1 or A0 layouts, which are the largest sizes in the editor. Tall shapes suit a city built along a spine, and the 50 × 70 cm (B2) is a mid-sized gallery option.

Which layout suits a very small town?

A tall layout with plenty of whitespace, such as a phone wallpaper or A4 Portrait. A small place has fewer roads, so a calmer canvas shows them off.

Open the editor at maparte.com, search for the place that matters to you, and try three layouts in a row: a tall one, a square one and a wide one. The 4K preview will show you at once which shape does your city justice.
