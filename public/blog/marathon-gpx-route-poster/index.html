Anyone who has trained for a long run knows that the race is only the last day of a long story. There were the early mornings, the first time you managed ten kilometres without stopping, the wet Sunday you nearly stayed in bed, the week the knee complained. On race day you cross a city on your own feet, and a few hours later it is over. A medal goes in a drawer. A photo goes into a folder. What is missing is a picture of the thing you actually did: the line you drew across a city with your own legs.

A route poster is that picture. It takes the track recorded by your watch or phone and draws it over a beautiful map of the place. The streets fade into a quiet texture, and your route glows on top, from the start line to the finish. It works for a marathon, a half marathon, a 10 km, a trail run, a long ride, a pilgrimage, a charity walk or a weekend loop around your own neighbourhood.

Why a route poster beats a medal in a drawer

There are good reasons that route posters have become one of the most popular personal map gifts.

It shows what you did, not just that you did it. A medal says "finished". A route poster shows the shape of the course: the long straight along the river, the loop through the park, the hill you remember with a shudder.

It is unique. Two runners in the same race run almost the same line, but a poster made from your own track is yours, down to the small wobble at the water station.

It makes a good gift. For a runner, a cyclist or a hiker, a poster of their own route is a thoughtful present, because it honours their effort. It also suits someone who is hard to buy for.

It looks like art. A bright line over a dark city looks like a drawing. People who have never run a step will still stop and look at it.

It works as a wallpaper too. Because the file is digital, you can make a tall phone version of the same route and see it every day.

What is a GPX file?

GPX is a simple, open file format for recorded tracks. It is a text file that lists a long series of points, each with a latitude, a longitude and usually a time and an elevation. Most sports watches, bike computers and phone running apps can record one, and most let you export it. Because the format is open, many other tools can read it.

You do not need to understand the contents. What matters is where to find the file and how to check that it is valid.

Where to get your GPX file

The exact steps differ between devices, so look in the help of your watch or app. In general, there are three routes.

  1. Export from the app. Open the activity in your sports app, look for an export or download option, and choose GPX. This is the most common way.
  2. Export from the watch maker's website. Many watch makers have a website where activities can be downloaded as files, with GPX among the options.
  3. Use a planned route. If you have not run yet, or if you want to make a poster of a route you plan to run, you can create a route in a route-planning tool and export it as GPX.

Most races also publish the official course as a GPX file or as a map. If you cannot find your own track, the course file is a fair substitute, though it will show the ideal line and not your wobbles.

Checking the file

Before you upload, check three things.

  • The extension is .gpx. Some apps export other formats such as FIT or TCX. Those need converting first.
  • The file is under 10 MB. The Maparte editor accepts GPX files up to 10 MB. Most single activities are far smaller than that. A very long multi-day track, or a file with extra data, may be larger.
  • It has track points. A damaged file, or one with no usable track points, cannot be added.

If the route looks wrong once it is in the editor, the file may come from a device that records in an unusual way. It is worth trying a second export from the same activity, or trying the course file instead.

Privacy: think about the start

A track records where you were, and that includes where you live. If your run starts and ends at your front door, the poster will show your address as its start marker. That is fine for a poster that stays on your own wall. If you plan to post it publicly, think about whether you want strangers to see where you live. You can trim the first and last few hundred metres in many export tools, or choose a start marker style that is less prominent, or share the poster only with people you know.

Choosing the story

A route poster can tell several stories. Decide which one you are telling, because it affects the zoom, the theme and the words.

  • The race. A marathon, a half marathon or a 10 km. The poster is a memento. The race name, the date and the finishing time are the details.
  • The training. A favourite loop run a hundred times, in the same park or along the same river. The poster is a portrait of a habit.
  • The journey. A long walk, a pilgrimage, a coast-to-coast ride or a week on a trail. The poster shows the line across a region, and the wide map is part of the story.
  • The gift. A poster for a friend or a family member who ran a race, with their name and time.
  • The plan. A route you have not done yet. Some people make the poster before the race, as a goal, and hang it where they will see it during the last weeks of training.

Race, or no race?

You do not need a race. A route you ran on a Sunday morning is as good a subject as a marathon. The poster does not judge the distance. What matters is that the line means something to you.

Framing: how much map around the route?

The most important choice on a route poster is the zoom. Too close and the route runs off the edge. Too far and it shrinks to a squiggle in a large empty map.

Here are some guidelines.

  • Fit the whole route with a margin. Zoom out until the entire route is visible, with a clear band of map around it. Leave a little more room at the bottom if you plan to put text there.
  • Think about the shape. A long, thin route, such as an out-and-back along a river, suits a tall layout more naturally than a square one. A compact loop fits a square. A route that sprawls across a city fits a landscape or a wide poster.
  • Include context. A river, a coastline, a park or a well-known district tells the viewer where the route is. If the route hugs a coast, make sure the coast is in the frame.
  • Do not crop the finish. The finish is the best part of the story. Check that the end of the line is not hidden behind the text area or the border.
  • Look at it small. Zoom out and squint. A good route poster is readable as a thumbnail: you can see the line and the shape.

If you upload a track that lies far from the area you had framed, the editor tells you so. Adding the route zooms the poster out to fit everything, or you can replace your existing routes with the new one to keep a tighter view. That is a helpful nudge if you first framed a city and then upload the track, and you can always adjust the view afterwards.

A marathon in a wide city

A marathon is 42.195 kilometres. It usually crosses a large part of a city, so the framing has to be generous. The route becomes a long ribbon, and the streets around it recede into a texture. Include the river or the coast if there is one, because it gives the ribbon a landmark to wrap around. A wide city view also looks better from a distance, which suits a large print.

A shorter run in a small area

A 5 km loop in a park can fill the frame at a much closer zoom. The streets are visible, and the park shows as a green shape. The route is thick and clear. A close view suits a smaller print or a phone wallpaper.

A route that is out and back

If your route goes out and returns by the same path, the line is drawn once, and the start and finish markers sit at the same end or close to it. Make sure the two markers do not overlap awkwardly. You can change the icons and sizes so that they sit side by side.

Choosing a theme that makes the line sing

The theme sets the map's colours, and on a route poster it has one extra job: it has to make the route stand out. A route is a single line, and the map behind it is made of thousands of lines. If they have similar colours and weights, the route disappears. If they contrast, the route glows.

Maparte has 21 themes. For a route poster, think in terms of contrast.

Dark themes with a bright line. Midnight Blue, Noir, Blueprint, Neon and Aurora have dark backgrounds, so a bright route in orange, yellow, pink or white leaps out. This is the most dramatic look and the one most people choose for a marathon. On a screen it looks like a glowing thread.

Light themes with a strong line. Carrara, Pencil Sketch, Sandstone and Blush have soft backgrounds. Here a deep colour such as red, black or navy makes the route readable, and the poster feels calm and elegant. It suits a living room or a hallway.

Water-led themes. Ocean and Blue Water make a coast or a river the hero. If your route runs along water, these give it a wonderful backdrop. Choose a warm route colour, such as orange or coral, against the cool blue.

Natural themes. Sage and other green-leaning themes work for trail runs and park loops, with a route in a warm colour for contrast.

Playful and artistic themes. Crayon Art, Pop Art, Chalkboard Cafe, Watercolor Wash, Japanese Ink and Retro Vintage have a personality of their own. Try them for a relaxed or humorous poster, but check that the route still stands out.

A quick test

Set the view, upload the route, and flip through five or six themes without changing anything else. For each, ask: can I see the route from across the room? Step back from the screen and look. The theme where the line is clear from the furthest distance, while the poster still looks good, is the winner.

If a theme you love makes the line hard to read, you can change the route colour to something that contrasts. That is often all it takes.

Styling the route

Once the route is on the map, the Routes panel gives you control over how it looks.

Colour. The default route colour is a warm red-orange, and you can change it with the colour picker. Pick a colour that contrasts with the map. Warm colours such as orange, red and yellow stand out against dark or blue backgrounds. Dark colours stand out against pale ones.

Line width. You can set the width from 1 to 12. A thin line looks elegant and delicate. A thick line is easier to read from a distance. For a poster that will hang on a wall, a medium-to-thick line is a good start. For a small phone wallpaper, a medium line is usually enough. A short, compact route can take a thicker line than a very long one.

Opacity. You can lower the opacity so the map shows through. A fully opaque line is bold. A slightly transparent line looks softer and lets overlapping parts show up, which is useful for a route that crosses itself.

Line style. You can choose solid or dashed. A dashed line suits a planned route, a trail or a journey. A solid line suits a recorded run.

Start and finish markers. Each route has a start marker and a finish marker. By default, the start is a circle and the finish is a flag. You can change the icons, the colours and the sizes. Large, clear endpoints help the eye follow the story from beginning to end.

Distance. The route card shows the length of the route, which is useful as a quick check that the right file has been loaded. If you know your distance, compare it. A marathon is 42.195 km, and a recorded track will often be a little longer, because of the way the watch measures and the line you actually ran.

Several routes on one poster

The editor accepts more than one route, and you can show or hide all of them with one control. That makes several nice posters possible.

  • A year of training. Layer several runs around the same park.
  • Two people, two lines. Each of a pair of runners in a different colour.
  • A multi-day journey. Each day as a separate route.
  • Before and after. A planned line and the line you ran.

Keep the colours distinct and the number small. Two or three routes are readable. Ten becomes a scribble.

Markers and landmarks

Besides the start and finish, you can add other markers to the map. Maparte's Markers panel offers built-in icons, emoji and custom icon uploads. Useful marker ideas for a route poster:

  • The place you had your first drink of water.
  • The spot where the hill begins.
  • The kilometre where you hit the wall.
  • A cheering family or a friend.
  • The bar where you celebrated.

Keep them few and small. The route is the star. One or two markers, besides start and finish, give the poster a human touch.

Writing the words

A route poster has a few facts that matter, and you should check each against your official result.

Title. The race name, the city, or a phrase of your own. "Berlin Marathon", "Sunday long run", "My first 10K".

Subtitle. The date, the distance, or the place.

Details. The finishing time, the distance, the pace, the elevation or the date. Choose the ones that matter to you. A poster does not need a table of statistics.

A personal line. "Never again", followed by a date a year later. A line that makes you smile when you read it in five years.

Some tone ideas, as examples to adapt.

Plain. The race name, the date and the time, in a clean typeface.

Proud. "42.195 km", "Finisher", and the year.

Playful. "Dad ran. Mum cheered. Dog barked." "Legs: tired. Heart: full."

Quiet. Just the route, the city and the date.

Check every figure

A wrong finishing time on a poster is hard to forgive. Before you download:

  • Compare the time with your official result, not your memory.
  • Check the date and the spelling of the race name.
  • Read each number twice.
  • Check the distance against the official distance, and decide whether you want to show the official figure or your recorded one.
  • Ask someone else to read it.

About times and bragging

You are allowed to be proud. A poster is for your wall and your pleasure. If the time is not what you hoped for, remember that finishing is the hard part, and consider leaving the time off and letting the route speak.

How a route becomes a line on a map

Understanding what happens to your file helps you get the best result.

A GPX track is a list of points recorded at intervals, often every second or every few seconds. When you upload it, the editor reads those points, works out where they fall on the map and joins them with a line. The distance on the route card is calculated from the points. If the device recorded a point every second, the line follows your actual path closely. If the device recorded less often, the line can look slightly angular on curves, and the distance may differ from the official figure.

Why your distance differs from the official one. A marathon is 42.195 kilometres along the shortest path a runner can take. Most runners cover a little more, because of the lines they take around other people, the corners they cut or do not cut, and the way a watch smooths its signal. A recorded track of 42.6 or 43 kilometres is normal. If you want to show the official distance on the poster, use it, and be clear in your own mind that the line shows what you ran.

Why the line sometimes wanders. Satellite signals bounce between tall buildings and can make a track look a little off the road. In a dense city centre or under bridges, you may see small jumps. It is part of the character of a recorded track, and many people like it. If a jump is large, try the official course file instead.

Elevation and pace are not shown. The poster draws the shape of the route over the map. It does not show hills or speed. If those matter to your story, mention them in a text line, such as "Total climb" with a figure you have checked.

Races, courses and rules

If you are making a poster of an organised race, a few practical points apply.

  • Check the course. Courses sometimes change from year to year. Use the file for the year you ran.
  • Use official facts. The official results page is the source for your time and your finishing position. Use those, not your watch.
  • Race names. Using a race's name as a title on your own personal poster, for your own wall, is a normal, personal use. If you plan to sell or distribute posters, that is a different matter, and you should ask the organisers first. Maparte's terms are for personal, non-commercial use.
  • Keep it factual. A title such as "My first marathon" with the city and the date is clear and needs no logo.

Training posters and progress

Not every route poster is about a race. Some of the best are about the habit of running.

A year in one park. Upload a handful of the runs you did around the same park, each as a thin, slightly transparent line. Where they overlap, the colour deepens, and the favourite paths glow. The effect is a portrait of a year.

Before and after. A first slow 5 km and a recent 10 km on the same map, in two colours. The caption can say just the two dates.

A plan on the wall. Before a big race, put the planned course on your wall as a poster. After the race, make a second poster with your real line, and hang the two together. The difference between plan and reality is a nice story in itself.

Making a poster for someone else

If you are making a route poster as a gift, you need a few things from the runner.

  1. The GPX file, or the race. Ask them to share the activity file, or find the official course.
  2. The details. Their name, the race, the date and the official time. Check them yourself.
  3. Their taste. Do they like dark or light, bold or calm? Look at what they wear and what hangs in their home.
  4. The format. A phone wallpaper, a printed poster or both.

If it is a surprise, you can often get the file from a shared activity or from a family member who follows their training. Be careful with privacy: a route can show where a person lives, so keep the file to yourself and share only the finished poster.

Seven routes, seven approaches

Here are seven scenarios, with the choices that suit each. The people, times and places are invented to show how the decisions work. They are illustrations, not real customers.

One: the first marathon, in a wide city

A runner finished a first marathon through a large city with a river. The poster uses a tall layout and a wide view, so that the whole course is visible as a long ribbon with the river beside it. A deep Midnight Blue theme, a thick orange line, a circle at the start and a flag at the finish. The title is the race name, the subtitle is the date and the distance, and the detail line is the finishing time, checked against the official result. It hangs in the hallway, where the runner passes it every morning on the way out.

Two: the half marathon by the sea

A coastal route along a promenade. The Ocean theme makes the sea the hero, and a warm coral line runs along the shore. The layout is landscape, because the route is long and thin. Start and finish markers sit at each end of the promenade. A caption with the date and "Sea, wind, finish".

Three: the park loop

A runner's favourite 5 km loop, run three times a week. A close view shows the park as a green shape, with the route as a bold line. A light theme and a deep red line. The caption reads "Three laps a week, fifty-two weeks". It is a portrait of a habit, not a race.

Four: the charity walk

A group of friends walked 20 km for a cause. The poster shows the whole route in a soft theme with a dashed line, which suits a walk. Each friend's name in the caption, and the amount raised, if they want to share it. It works as a thank-you for the group, and each can print a copy.

Five: the long ride

A cyclist rode across a region over two days. The poster is wide and shows a large area: coast, hills and a few towns. Each day is a separate route in a different colour. The thin line style looks elegant at this scale. Because the area is large, the roads become a quiet texture, and the line shows the shape of the journey.

Six: the trail run

A trail route through hills. A natural theme with a strong, warm line, a start marker at the trailhead and a finish marker at the summit. A few markers for key points, such as a hut or a viewpoint. The caption has the distance and the date.

Seven: the surprise gift

A sister makes a poster for her brother, who has just finished his first race. She asks him for the GPX file, or finds it in the race's official course. She picks a dark theme with a bright route, adds his name, the race name, the date and his official time. She sends the file that evening, and promises a printed version, which she arranges at a local shop. He sees the line he ran, the whole course on one page, and realises how far it was.

Giving a route poster

How you give the poster matters as much as the poster.

As a file, on the day. A message with the image and a line of congratulation is a nice end to race day. Because the file exists the moment you download it, you can send it before the runner has taken off their shoes.

As a wallpaper. Choose the phone layout and send it with a note. The route becomes part of their everyday life.

Printed and framed. Take the file to a print shop of your choice, or print it at home for small sizes. Choose a matte paper for a quiet look. We do not print or ship posters, so this step is yours.

As a pair. Two runners each make a poster of the same race, with their own route and time, and hang them side by side.

With a medal. A medal hung on the frame, or a small shelf beneath, turns the poster into a small display.

As a team gift. A club or a group of friends can each add a route, as separate lines in different colours, to make a poster of a shared day.

Printing it yourself, briefly

If you want paper, a few practical points help.

  • Send the file at its original size. Do not resize or crop it.
  • Ask for a small test print first. Bright route colours can look calmer on paper than on screen, and dark backgrounds can look heavier.
  • Choose a matte paper for a quiet look, and a satin or semi-gloss paper if you want the colours to feel deeper.
  • Choose the frame first, then design to its proportions.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • A route that is too thin. A line that looks bold on a screen can vanish on a wall. Increase the width, and step back to check.
  • A theme where the line disappears. Choose contrast, and change the route colour if needed.
  • A tight crop. Leave a margin around the route. A line that touches the edge looks cramped.
  • A hidden finish. Make sure the finish marker is not covered by the text or the border.
  • Too many markers. Start, finish and one or two more are enough.
  • Wrong figures. Check the time, the distance and the date against your official result.
  • An exposed home address. Think before you publish a poster whose start is your front door.
  • A mismatched layout. A square layout for a very long, thin route wastes space. Try a tall or wide layout.
  • Skipping the full-size check. The 4K preview exists for a reason. Zoom into the start, the finish and the thin lines.

A checklist before you download

  1. The GPX file is the right activity, and the distance on the route card looks right.
  2. The whole route is visible, with a margin.
  3. The line is clear from a distance, and the theme gives it contrast.
  4. The start and finish markers are clear and do not overlap.
  5. The race name, date and time are spelt and checked against the official result.
  6. No text is cut off by the border or the edge.
  7. The data credit is present and legible.
  8. The layout matches the frame or the screen.
  9. You have looked at the preview at full size.
  10. You have saved the file in two places and opened the downloaded file.

Frequently asked questions

What is a GPX file?

A GPX file is a simple text file that records a track: a series of points with latitude, longitude and usually a time. Most sports watches and running apps can export one.

How big can the GPX file be?

The editor accepts GPX files under 10 MB. Most single activities are well below that limit.

What if my route is rejected?

A damaged file, or one with no usable track points, cannot be added. Try exporting the activity again, or use the official course file for the race.

Can I add more than one route?

Yes. Several routes can be shown at once, and you can show or hide them all with one control. Use distinct colours and keep the number small.

Can I change the colour and thickness of the line?

Yes. You can change the colour, the width, the opacity and the line style, which can be solid or dashed. You can also change the start and finish markers.

What if I do not have a GPX file?

Use markers for the start and the finish, and choose a view that shows the area. Many races also publish their official course as a file or a map.

Does Maparte print or ship posters?

No. Maparte provides digital files only. You download a file and use it on a screen, share it or take it to a printer of your choice.

Which file format should I choose?

PNG is a safe general choice. JPG is smaller, TIFF and PDF are popular with print shops, and SVG is a vector format for very large uses. The live download panel lists the current formats.

Can I make a phone wallpaper of my route?

Yes. Maparte has wallpaper layouts, including four iPhone layouts and a Galaxy layout. For the iPhone 16/17 Pro and iPhone 17, the layout is 1206 x 2622 pixels.

Is the route exact?

The poster draws the points in your file over the map, so it shows the track your device recorded. Devices differ in how precisely they record. The poster is a work of art and is not meant for navigation.

Who owns the map data?

The map data comes from OpenStreetMap contributors, and the credit appears on the poster. Keep it legible.

Can I sell the posters?

No. Maparte's terms allow personal, non-commercial use. Check the live terms for the current position.

Final thoughts

A route poster takes a day, or a season, of effort and gives it a shape. Export the track, frame the whole route with a margin, choose a theme that gives the line contrast, style the line so it reads from across a room, write only words you have checked and look at the preview at full size.

When you are ready, open the Maparte editor at maparte.com, search for your city and upload your GPX file. If you want help with colours, read our guide to the themes. The best route poster is not the fastest time or the biggest race. It is the line that means something to the person who ran it.
